# Jamming of VHF voice nets in the mid-1980s: research (October 2026)

The research behind roadmap 16.0 (its voice-jamming half) and 16.2 (voice jamming carried by the enemy's packages, and
the nets' drills in answer). A research workflow of 31 Oct 2026 put four questions to the sources: what NATO had against
voice nets, what the Warsaw Pact had, what jamming did to an FM voice net, and what the record of practice shows. A
sceptic then checked each finding against its source. Only findings the sceptics found supported, or partly supported
(with the caveat stated), are used here. The source keys are registered in `docs/SOURCES.md` under "Voice jamming
sources". The crew-side procedure already researched in `docs/research/CREW_COMMS_2026-10.md` ("Against jamming the
drill was report, spare, repeat, relay") is referred to, not repeated.

In a European war of 1985 an Osa battery's VHF voice nets would have been jammed **from the ground near the front, not
by the package that attacked it**. On NATO's side, the jammers aimed at air defence nets were the US Army's. Soviet
authors credited a group of **15-18 ground jammers of 1-2 kW with reaching 30 km into the tactical depth**, and the
**Quick Fix helicopter with 2-76 MHz out to 60 km** ([palii_1989, USSR TT][palii-22]; [p. 23][palii-23]). The
**EF-111A jammed radars only**. The **EC-130H Compass Call**, the one airborne communications jammer, was aimed mostly at
fighter control and **had no squadron in Europe until 1 October 1986**. On the Pact's side, doctrine gave tactical VHF jamming to **army
ground battalions set up 10-15 km behind the line, reaching about 25 km** ([stemasov_1969, USSR OD][stem]). They were
switched on with the strike and aimed by name at the communications of anti-aircraft command posts and at warning nets
([gs_lesson12_1977, USSR OD][l12]); no Pact-side source puts a voice jammer in an air package. The physics is simple:
an FM receiver follows whichever signal is about twice as strong, so a jammer's reach grows with its power and shrinks
with the friendly link's length and the roughness of the ground ([fm_34_40_7, USA OD][fm34407]). The only war account
of SAM voice nets being jammed is the Bekaa on 9 June 1982. There the warning and control nets were jammed from the
strike's take-off and the radars on the approach, and **the jamming was eased a few minutes before the strike so as not
to hinder the anti-radiation missiles** ([paprcka_1984, ČSSR ST][paprcka]; [ben_dor_2016, Israel VA][bendor]). The
jammed side answered with procedure: keep working, log and report, say commands twice, move to the spare frequency on
the main station's order, relay ([rrs_1986, USSR OD][rrs]; [strohschein_1980, GDR ST][stroh]). For SAM3D's campaigns
as they stand, battery 21 at Kesmark lies about 130 km from the Pact's line and 170 km from NATO's, beyond every ground
or helicopter jammer's reach: voice jamming touches it only when a task puts it near one of those fronts.

*How to read the citations.* Each citation gives the source's key in `docs/SOURCES.md`, its country and its type, and
links the page read:

- **OD**: official document (regulation, manual, standard, staff lecture, fact sheet, archive record).
- **TT**: textbook or training text.
- **ST**: study, staff journal article or thesis.
- **VA**: veteran's or participant's account.
- **PR**: press or magazine article.
- **RP**: reference page (encyclopaedia, designation list, enthusiast or heritage site).

A NATO document about the Pact counts only for what NATO believed, and a Soviet one about NATO only for what the
Soviets believed. Original-language terms are quoted in «». Every inference is labelled as one. The appendix registers
each source with its date, tier and caveats.

## 1. NATO against the voice nets in 1985

### 1.1 The US Army's jammers were the ones aimed at air defence nets

Palii's 1989 book gives the Soviet reading of NATO's communications jammers ([palii_1989, USSR TT][palii-22];
[p. 23][palii-23]):

- the **AN/MLQ-34 (TACJAM)**: 20-150 MHz, up to three links (nets or radio directions) per set;
- the **AN/TLQ-17A**: HF and VHF, 1.5-60 MHz (the upper bound partly garbled in the OCR), on a 1.25 t truck with trailer,
  an M113 or an EH-1H;
- the helicopter-borne **AN/ALQ-151 Quick Fix-2**, working with the TSQ-114 ground system: HF and VHF, **2-76 MHz, out to
  60 km**;
- a **group of 15-18 ground jammers of 1-2 kW**, against HF and VHF links and radars **in the tactical depth out to
  30 km**. `CREW_COMMS_2026-10.md` reads it as a corps group; the page itself says only "the group".
- a **155 mm shell carrying six expendable VHF barrage jammers**, working **for a few minutes out to 20 km**. Palii does
  not say it was fielded. Elsewhere he gives foreign expendable jammers 10 minutes to 2 hours of work, one example
  about 60 minutes ([p. 8][palii-8]).

US divisional EW battalions, he writes, jam «прежде всего» (first of all) the systems of reconnaissance, of ground
artillery fire control and of troop air defence («войсковой ПВО»). Air defence is among the first-priority targets,
listed third ([p. 22][palii-22]). NATO air forces' ground EW groups, wings and regiments exist «для выявления и
подавления помехами КВ/УКВ радиосвязи систем ПВО и ВВС», to find and jam the HF/VHF radio of air defence and air force
systems ([p. 27][palii-27]).

These bands cover the R-123M (20-51.5 MHz), the R-111 and the R-107 (20-52 MHz) entirely. The R-173 (30-76 MHz) runs
above the TLQ-17A's 60 MHz (inference from the bands).

US-side evidence of what was in service by 1985 is thin:

- The AN/MLQ-34 was in a human-factors evaluation before its low-rate production decision in 1979
  ([dtic_quickfix_tacjam, USA OD][dtic-tacjam]; catalogue record only).
- The EH-1H Quick Fix flew a preliminary airworthiness evaluation with the AN/ARQ-33 in August 1977 (catalogued October
  1977) ([dtic_quickfix_tacjam, USA OD][dtic-qf]). An airworthiness test is not fielding.
- FM 34-40-7's equipment list of 1992 gives the AN/TLQ-17A(V)3 as 1.5-80 MHz and 550 W, the AN/ULQ-19 as 20-80 MHz, 100 W
  FM, and the AN/TLQ-15 as 1.5-20 MHz HF, 2 kW ([fm_34_40_7, USA OD][fm34407]). That is 1992 equipment.

No source found gives fielding dates in Europe by 1985. Palii's Quick Fix-2 is the late-1980s EH-60A, whose date of
reaching units is not confirmed by an acceptable source. If a Quick Fix flew over a 1985 battlefield, it was most likely
the EH-1H (inference).

The US Army's division EW manual of 1986, FM 34-10, is reported to name "enemy air defense communications nets" among
Quick Fix's targets and air defence C3 nets among the ground jamming teams' ([fm_34_10_1986, USA OD][fm3410]). The
checkers could not open the FOIA scan, so nothing from it is used until it is read.

Palii describes NATO's methods ([p. 24][palii-24]):

- **massed**: simultaneous suppression, for a set time, of the most dangerous systems, mainly in the offensive;
- **selective**: sequential suppression after careful reconnaissance, most effective in defence;
- **concentrated-selective**: the two combined, together with fire strikes.

US exercises of the early 1980s, he adds, found jamming most effective in the tactical zone.

### 1.2 Compass Call: a communications jammer, not in Europe, aimed at fighter control

**Dates.** First flight 1981, delivery 1982, initial operating capability 1983 ([dvids_2018_ec130h, USA OD][dvids]).
Delivery began on 19 March 1982, and the combat debut was Panama in 1989 ([afmag_ec130h, USA PR][afmag-ec130h]). In June
1987 the USAF had ten, with six more to come that year ([correll_1987, USA PR][correll]). The article does not name
their squadron or base.

**Where.** USAFE's electronic combat organisation was formed on 1 June 1985: the 65th Air Division and the 66th
Electronic Combat Wing at Sembach. Its Compass Call squadron, the 43d ECS, was activated there only on 1 October 1986
and flew the EC-130H from 1987 to 1991 ([wiki_usafe_ew_units, USA RP][wiki-43]; [65th Air Division][wiki-65]). These
dates come through Wikipedia from the AFHRA lineage factsheets, which could not be opened. **In a 1985 war, a Compass Call would have to
come from the United States** (inference).

**What for.** The sources move from a broad purpose to a narrow one:

- In 1981 it was to disrupt "selected portions of the Soviet force management system through standoff jamming", causing
  "loss of control over Soviet tactical airpower" ([ulsamer_1981, USA PR][ulsamer]).
- In 1987 it was "a communications jammer that would work from standoff range against the enemy's command and control
  net" ([correll_1987, USA PR][correll]).
- A 1994 Air University study says it was "for the most part" a jammer of "the air-to-air and air-to-ground
  communications employed by enemy airborne interceptors" ([hutcherson_1994, USA ST][hutch]). That means **GCI and
  fighter-control voice, not SAM battery nets** (inference). The same study says the Compass Call, EF-111 and F-4G
  communities worked as separate stovepipes until the 65th Air Division trained them together. Its author's view that
  before 1989 the allies "did not target the command and control of enemy forces to prevent escalation" conflicts with
  the 1981 purpose. It is recorded as his opinion, low weight.

**How tasked.** Through the air tasking order: target priorities, objective areas and timing, restricted frequencies.
"COMPASS CALL is not a tethered jammer controlled from the ground", and its concepts of employment were still "undergoing
refinement" in 1991 ([fm_90_24_1991, USA OD][fm9024]). In 1989 the USAF still stressed that Weasels, Ravens and Compass
Call "have to be used together" ([afmag_1989_slam, USA PR][slam]).

**Not found:** its 1980s frequency coverage (whether it reached the 20-52 MHz band at all), its power and its stand-off
distance.

### 1.3 The EF-111A jammed radars only

Every source agrees on this point:

- The EF-111A could "disrupt early warning and GCI radars", as a stand-off jammer or as an escort; 42 were built
  ([correll_1987, USA PR][correll]).
- Its role was barrier jamming from the friendly side of the front ([ulsamer_1981, USA PR][ulsamer]).
- The Soviet reading gives it ten noise and responsive jamming stations against early-warning, guidance and
  target-designation radars and SAM and AAA control. From over its own territory it could jam several ground radars at
  once **175-200 km from the line of contact**. Palii counts 36 in USAF service by mid-1987
  ([palii_1989, USSR TT][palii-27]).

The sources disagree on its entry into service: a planned IOC of November 1983 ([ulsamer_1981][ulsamer]) against
"operational since 1981" ([correll_1987][correll]). For Europe the date that matters is the 42d ECS at RAF Upper
Heyford, which flew it from 1984 ([wiki_usafe_ew_units, USA RP][wiki-42]).

The EA-6B is listed against "radar, electronic data links and communications", the last with the USQ-113 of later
blocks ([usn_ea6b_2003, USA OD][ea6b]). Whether it jammed voice nets in 1985 is not known. At El Dorado Canyon (1986),
Palii has it jamming radars only (§5.2).

## 2. What the Warsaw Pact had

### 2.1 Army jamming battalions near the front

A General Staff Academy lecture of 1969 sets out the front's jamming forces ([stemasov_1969, USSR OD][stem]):

- A front had 2-3 SPETSNAZ-A radio battalions to jam the enemy's HF, VHF («ультракоротковолновой») and radio-relay
  communications at the tactical level. They were attached to the armies on the main axes: an army on the axis of the
  main attack "may receive" one for the operation (not "normally had one").
- The front also had 1-2 SPETSNAZ-F battalions (operational HF), an RVGK battalion, a helicopter radio squadron against
  radio-relay links, and SPETSNAZ-S radiotechnical battalions against airborne radars and aircraft guidance links.
- The battalions set up **10 to 15 km from the line of contact** and may move two or three times a day. **The companies
  that jam VHF move in the battle formations of the first-echelon divisions.**
- Table 2 (OCR garbled but legible) gives a SPETSNAZ-A battalion **a VHF jamming range of about 25 km, 12-15 km in
  barrage mode**. A front with three such battalions jams up to 60 nets, or 150-450 VHF channels in barrage.
- The front plans to neutralise about 150 HF, 250 VHF and 150 radio-relay links, and to disrupt **30 to 35 tactical
  aviation and air defence control and warning centres and posts**.
- Jamming of missile-unit, tactical-aviation, first-echelon and warning nets begins "simultaneously with the initiation
  of the front nuclear strike".
- Neutralising the enemy's air defence radioelectronic system cuts the front aviation's losses fourfold to fivefold.

This is 1969 doctrine for an operation opened with nuclear strikes.

A General Staff Academy lesson of 1977 names the air defence targets ([gs_lesson12_1977, USSR OD][l12]):

- "As a first step it is advantageous to neutralize ... the communications centers of the control posts (command posts)
  of the antiaircraft battalions, the radars ... and also the radio communications for warning of large units and for the
  fire control of antiaircraft means" (p. 7). The target set is a NATO corps.
- Army separate "N" battalions and the "S" companies of ECM battalions jam VHF and short-range radio navigation. The
  translator glosses N and S as intercept. While the forward security zone is being crossed they reconnoitre "in
  readiness to produce jamming"; they do not jam yet.
- In non-nuclear combat jamming is used "by surprise and massively on selected axes". In the breakthrough it is "strictly
  coordinated in time with the preparatory fire", and it includes the radio that warns the enemy's large units.
- Jamming together with fire keeps aviation losses at 5-7 % or less; jamming alone cuts them five- or sixfold.

The 1984 tactics textbook makes electronic warfare a kind of combat support, "implemented concurrently with the
destruction of the most important enemy electronic targets" ([reznichenko_1984, USSR TT][rez]).

NATO's view agrees on the echelons:

- In 1984 a front had a radio and radar intercept regiment, a radio intercept regiment, an **air defence jamming
  regiment** and a **radio jamming regiment**, and armies had battalion-size EW units. They would jam "at critical times"
  ([fm_100_2_1_1984, USA OD][fm10021]).
- In 1991 FM 100-2-3's front chart still shows both jamming regiments. Its combined arms army chart shows radio and
  radiotechnical reconnaissance battalions and **no jamming unit** ([fm100_2_3, USA OD][fm10023]).

Russian press histories give the lineage (tier 4):

- The first post-war communications jamming battalion was formed in 1954, with experimental HF («Овод») and VHF
  («Перевал») jammers.
- New ground jammers, «Тобол» among them, came in 1965-1990. New front and army unit sets followed the exercises from
  Эфир-72 to Импульс-76.
- In the summer 1968 exercise «Днепр», jamming held the 16th Tank Army on a bridgehead for eight hours and made the 10th
  Army more than two hours late. In 1957 troop air defence received a battalion that jammed radars and aviation VHF radio
  ([vpk_2004_reb, Russia PR][vpk]).
- Jamming battalions were formed in all the services in 1956-59, and the REB service was built up in 1968-73. In local
  wars, radar and radio jamming was "as a rule" coordinated with air and artillery strikes
  ([nvo_2003_reb, Russia PR][nvo]).
- A veterans'-site page quoting Burakov dates the R-325, R-325M, R-330 and R-330A to the 1960s, and the REB unit sets of
  front, army and army corps to 1976-77 ([ruzhany_reb, Russia RP][ruzhany]).

For a non-Soviet Pact army nothing usable was found. The Bundesarchiv's "Funk- und Funktechnisches Störbataillon 18"
(1976-87) is a Volksmarine (navy) unit. An NVA regulation of 1985 on radioelectronic combat in operations and
engagements, DV 046/0/007, exists but was not read ([barch_dv046, GDR OD][barch-dv]).

### 2.2 The jammers and their bands

- **R-330A**: FM 100-2-1 names it with the R-834 as the available radio jammers in 1984, with "newer and more
  technically-advanced jammers" coming ([fm_100_2_1_1984, USA OD][fm10021]). It is dated to the 1960s
  ([ruzhany_reb][ruzhany]). A designation list calls it «Перевал» on the ZiL-157, with the compiler's own question marks
  ([rwd_mb3_ng_r, Germany RP][rwdng]). **Its band and power were not found.**
- **R-330B «Мандат-Б»**: on the MT-LBu, **30-100 MHz, 1 kW** ([ru_wiki_r330, Russia RP][ruwiki330]). These figures carry
  no footnote, and the service-entry year was not found ("developed in the 1970s").
- **R-330U «Укол»**: accepted in 1985 (the date rests on a forum footnote), receives 30-100 MHz, **jams 30-60 MHz**, 1 kW
  ([ru_wiki_r330][ruwiki330]).
- **R-330P**: a communications jammer on the MT-LB, in NATO's view of 1991 ([fm100_2_3][fm10023]).
- **NATO's 1980 picture** of a Soviet VHF jammer: vehicular or airborne, **1,500-2,000 W, 30-76 MHz**, directional
  antennas, spot or barrage. Its jamming sounds are noise, gulls, tones, bagpipes, voices and pulses (a US staff college
  thesis, Table 3-1, compiled from Gordon 1975 and Fiedler 1979) ([williamson_1980, USA ST][will]). The 30-76 MHz band
  mirrors the US FM radios of the day.
- **HF jammers**: the R-325M covered 1.5-25.5 MHz at 5-7 kW pulsed, one selective HF link at the operational level,
  according to an anonymous Czech or Slovak veteran's service notes ([valka_r325m, ČSSR VA][r325m]).

### 2.3 Air-delivered communications jamming was weak on the Pact side

Here the two sides' sources disagree:

- **NATO**: FM 100-2-3 (1991) calls the Mi-8 **HIP K "a radio-jamming version with a large crossed-dipole antenna array
  on each side of the cabin"**, organic with the HIP J to the front's helicopter ECM squadron, and notes its power
  limitations ([fm100_2_3, USA OD][fm10023]). FM 100-2-1 (1984) gives a Mi-4 HOUND C "communication jammers"
  ([fm_100_2_1_1984][fm10021]), and a 1982 article has an ECM Mi-4 "first reported in 1977"
  ([lawrence_1982, USA PR][lawrence]).
- **Russian sources** describe the Soviet Mi-8 jammers as **radar jammers**: the Mi-8SMV (1971, «Смальта-В», against SAM
  radars), the Mi-8PP (1974, against ground detection, guidance and target-designation radars) and the Mi-8PPA (modified
  in 1980-82 by some accounts) ([ru_wiki_mi8, Russia RP][ruwikimi8]; these lines are unreferenced). The same page gives
  the Mi-8PP crossed dipole antennas on the fuselage sides: the feature NATO used to identify the HIP K.

The Soviet documents give helicopters only radio-relay jamming at the operational level: one helicopter radio squadron
per front in 1969 ([stemasov_1969][stem]). A *Военная мысль* article proposed more, but only as proposals:

- squadrons of 6-8 helicopters with four jammers each against radio relay;
- V-10P (Mi-6P) helicopters jamming from airborne alert zones;
- one-time VHF jammers, delivered by missile, shell or aircraft into enemy command posts' areas, which "there has long
  been a need to develop" ([katrechko_1976, USSR ST][katr]).

A veteran REB staff officer confirms that separate EW air regiments and separate EW helicopter squadrons for the group
protection of strike aviation existed until the cuts of 1980-90 ([lyubin_2009, Russia VA][lyubin]). He says nothing of
VHF voice jamming. A designation list adds an R-949V helicopter jammer and R-949Sh radio-relay jammers for the
Mi-8MTSh1-3, undated and of unknown band ([rwd_mb3_ng_r][rwdng]).

**SAM3D uses the Russian reading: the Pact's helicopter jammers of 1985 are radar jammers, and NATO's "HIP K radio
jammer" is recorded as the alternative.** The project's rule prefers sources from the operating countries, then the
jamming side's own account of its own equipment, and here both point the same way. A Pact package therefore brings
radar jammers, and the battery's voice nets are the target of the army's ground battalions, timed to the strike
(inference).

### 2.4 A band gap below 30 MHz (inference)

The R-330B (30-100 MHz), the R-330U (jams 30-60 MHz) and NATO's 1980 picture (30-76 MHz) all start at 30 MHz. The
R-123M, R-111 and R-107 work down to 20 MHz. A Pact enemy's VHF jammers of these types would therefore miss a net below
30 MHz:

- The PU-12M's FuG-2 (the covered unit's net, 20-27 MHz in the NVA's table: `CREW_COMMS_2026-10.md`) would lie below
  them.
- The battery's own net on FuG-4 (37-51.5 MHz) and the R-407 telecode (52-60 MHz) would lie inside them.

This is unconfirmed: the R-330A's band is unknown, and the HF jammers (the R-325M up to 25.5 MHz) overlap 20-25.5 MHz.
NATO's jammers, on Palii's bands, cover 20-30 MHz in any case.

## 3. The physics of jamming an FM voice net

### 3.1 Capture: the jammer needs about twice the signal at the receiver

The US Army's jamming handbook works its sums with **K = 2, "Number 2 for jamming frequency modulated receivers (jammer
tuning accuracy)"** ([fm_34_40_7, USA OD][fm34407]). Reading K as a jam-to-signal requirement of 2 (+3 dB) at the target
receiver is an interpretation, but a reasonable one. The same manual states the mechanism: the FM receiver "will be
captured" by the stronger signal, and "if the jamming-to-signal ratio is too small, then jamming will not be effective".
A US staff college thesis of 1980 gives the same 2:1 capture point ([williamson_1980, USA ST][will]). The handbook is
dated 23 November 1992: its rules are generic propagation, usable as a model but not as 1985 doctrine.

Palii says the same in Soviet terms. Jamming works where its power at the receiver «превышала или была соизмеримой с
сигналом» (exceeded or was comparable with the signal). The suppression zone is the region where the jam-to-signal
ratio exceeds a suppression coefficient Kп, and a smaller Kп means easier suppression ([palii_1989, USSR TT][palii-7];
[p. 8][palii-8]). No Soviet value of Kп for FM voice is legible in the OCR.

### 3.2 The distance law

The handbook's worked examples fit

`Pj = K · Pt · (Dj / Dt)^n · E`, so the jammer's greatest distance is `Dj = Dt · (Pj / (K · Pt · E))^(1/n)`,

where Pj is the jammer's power, Pt the enemy transmitter's, Dj the jammer-to-receiver distance, Dt the
transmitter-to-receiver distance, E an elevation factor and n a terrain exponent ([fm_34_40_7, USA OD][fm34407]). The
OCR garbles the formula, so its form is reconstructed from the examples, which it reproduces.

| n | Ground (FM 34-40-7) |
|---|---|
| 2 | level terrain, over water |
| 3 | rolling hills, farmland |
| 4 | moderately rough, rolling to high hills, forested farmland |
| 5 | very rough (rocky mountains, desert), poor conductivity |

**Siting.** "The jammer must be located at the same elevation above the sea level or higher than the enemy target
transmitter", with a "reasonable LOS propagation path" to the target receiver and "no high hills between". The
elevation factor comes from the ratio of the two sites' heights: it is 1 within 10 m, and a ratio of 0.75 gives 1.8
(Table A-7). The power is multiplied by it, or elsewhere the distance divided: a jammer at 390 m against a transmitter at
500 m loses a factor of 1.6 in range (Table A-12). A log-periodic antenna instead of the whip halves the power needed in
one formula, doubles it in another and doubles the distance in a third. The manual is inconsistent here.

**Its examples:**

1. At 37.5 MHz, a 5 W transmitter 9 km from its receiver, a jammer 17 km from the receiver and n = 4 give "Pj = 124.60 or
   125 watts" (62.5 W with the log-periodic antenna). The exact ratio gives 127.3 W: the manual rounded its steps.
2. A 1,500 W jammer can be at most **31.5 km** from the receiver with its whip (37.44 km with the log-periodic antenna).
   This reproduces exactly.
3. A 550 W AN/TLQ-17A(V)3 against a 2 W transmitter 3 km away can stand at most 5 km from the receiver with its whip
   after the elevation penalty, 10 km with the log-periodic antenna.

**Worked examples for SAM3D (inference).** These use the law above with K = 2 and E = 1, the R-123M at 20 W
(`data/em/osa_ak.json`, after `r123_refs`) and a 5 km link from the PU-12M to a launcher. The table gives the greatest
jammer-to-launcher distance at which the launcher's receiver is captured:

| Jammer | n = 4 | n = 3 |
|---|---|---|
| 550 W | 9.6 km | 12.0 km |
| 1 kW | 11.2 km | 14.6 km |
| 2 kW | 13.3 km | 18.4 km |
| 4 kW | 15.8 km | 23.2 km |

A 10 km link doubles every figure. Raising the transmitter's power fivefold (the R-111 from 20 % to 100 %) shrinks the
jammer's reach by 5^(1/n): to 0.67 at n = 4 and 0.58 at n = 3. The arithmetic was checked. The inputs are a 1992 US
planning rule applied to Soviet sets, so these figures are fit for game balance, not system data.

### 3.3 Receivers are jammed, not transmitters

"Jammers operate against receivers--not transmitters" ([fm_24_33_1990, USA OD][fm2433-3]). In Palii's geometry, when the
jammer's energy potential exceeds the transmitter's, the suppression zone covers the whole plane except a circle near
the transmitter, displaced away from the jammer ([palii_1989, USSR TT][palii-9]; the checker's reading of a noisy page).
Within one battery net, then, the receivers near the jammer and far from their transmitter go first. The net can become
one-way: launchers that no longer hear the PU-12M may still be heard by it (inference).

### 3.4 Spot, barrage and sweep

- **Spot** jamming "is concentrated power directed toward one channel or frequency". **Barrage** is power spread over
  several channels at once ([fm_24_33_1990, USA OD][fm2433-3]).
- "Barrage jamming needs high power to operate. It cannot distinguish between enemy and friendly frequencies." **Sweep**
  dwells a set time on each frequency in turn and also hits friendly ones ([ss0134_jamming, USA TT][ss0134]).
- NATO's 1980 example: 2 kW spread in barrage over 20 channels leaves 100 W per channel ([williamson_1980][will]).
- Spread over the R-123M's whole 20-51.5 MHz band (1,260 channels of 25 kHz), 1 kW leaves about 0.8 W per channel. By the
  law above, that captures the 5 km link's launcher only from about 1.9 km at n = 4 (inference). That is the likely
  reason barrage jammers were expendables dropped near the target (inference; Palii's 155 mm shell, §1.1).

**Signal-initiated jamming** jams pre-programmed frequencies automatically "as they become active", at a risk of
fratricide ([fm_34_40_7, USA OD][fm34407]). The Pact's staff article calls narrowband (spot) jamming the most effective:
the jammer joins the enemy's traffic, "mostly using the call signs of the stations whose traffic it enters"
([strohschein_1980, GDR ST][stroh]).

**How fast a spot jammer finds a net's spare frequency: no period figure from a tier 1-2 source.** The only number seen
was an HF jammer's retune time on a tier 5 forum, and it is not used. FM 34-10's sample tasking is reported to give each
net a primary and a second frequency, which would let a jammer be briefed on the spare in advance. That is unverified
(§1.1).

**Data before voice.** The 1986 manual warns that printing telegraph carries more traffic than aural telegraphy but is
"in greater measure subject to interference" ([rrs_1986, USSR OD][rrs] §93). It compares data with Morse by ear, not
with voice. A post-Soviet paper puts the suppression of keyed data links at jam-to-signal ratios of −11, −8 and −5 dB
(AM, FSK and PSK keying, at a bit error rate of 5·10⁻³), against about 0 dB in classical jamming theory
([dvornikov_2016, Belarus ST][dvornikov]). Those are model values without coding. **The R-407 telecode would fail before
the voice nets do** (inference).

### 3.5 The price of talking longer

The jammer is also a listener:

- Jammers are keyed in short bursts against direction finding, and "maximum jammer power output means maximum electronic
  visibility" ([fm_34_40_7, USA OD][fm34407]).
- In NATO's view the Soviets direction-found high-power VHF out to about 40 km, and a transmission of more than 20-25 s
  was the danger point ([fm_100_2_1_1984, USA OD][fm10021]).
- The US Army's own limit was 20 s, 15 s preferred. A 500 m circular error was "a very good" DF fix, and about 1,500 m
  was typical for a direction finder within 20-25 km of the line ([fm_24_18_1987, USA OD][fm2418]).

Under jamming a net repeats its commands and calls longer (§6), so jamming raises its exposure to direction finding
(inference). That matters for roadmap 16.3.

## 4. How jamming sounded and how it was graded

### 4.1 Obvious and subtle jamming

**Obvious jamming.** The US manuals of the period list the signals: random noise, stepped tones ("bagpipes"), random
keyed CW, random pulse, wobbler, tone, pulse, recorded sounds, sweep-through, rotary, spark and gulls
([fm_24_18_1987, USA OD][fm2418]). Gulls are "very effective against voice", and random noise is "similar to normal
background noise" ([fm_24_33_1990, USA OD][fm2433-3]).

**Subtle jamming.** An unmodulated carrier stronger than the friendly signal captures the receiver, which goes silent:
"squelch capture". The operator checks by switching the squelch and disconnecting the antenna, and reports every
suspected case even when he has overcome it ([fm_24_33_1990][fm2433-3]; [fm_24_18_1987][fm2418]). Palii describes the
same effect from the other side: masking can show as «исчезновение собственных шумов», the receiver's own noise
disappearing, "typical of VHF" ([palii_1989, USSR TT][palii-3], as read in `CREW_COMMS_2026-10.md`).

How the R-123M's noise suppressor (ШУМЫ) behaves under capture is not documented in any source found.

### 4.2 The Soviet grades

The 1986 radio manual grades voice reception on two five-point scales, audibility («ЩСА») and intelligibility («ЩРК»)
([rrs_1986, USSR OD][rrs] §25 and the code table, pp. 79-80). The intelligibility grades read:

| ЩРК | Signals are |
|---|---|
| 1 | «неразборчивы» (unintelligible) |
| 2 | «разборчивы временами» (intelligible at times) |
| 3 | «разборчивы с трудом» (intelligible with difficulty) |
| 4 | «разборчивы» (intelligible) |
| 5 | «вполне разборчивы» (fully intelligible) |

Voice is satisfactory and fit for operational traffic only at **ЩСА 3 or better and ЩРК 4 or better**. The grades are
"subjective, depending on the training and experience" of the operator. The audibility grades' words are not given here
because the extracted code table is misaligned. The manual came into force on 19 May 1986, a year after the game's war;
whether the 1965 manual before it had the same rule was not checked.

The Soviet state standard for radiotelephone speech sets five classes of intelligibility
([gost_16600_72, USSR OD][gost]):

| Class | Understood | Sounds, % | Monosyllabic words, % |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | with no strain | > 90 | > 95 |
| II | without difficulty | 85-90 | 92-95 |
| III | with strained attention, without asking again or repeats | 78-85 | 87-92 |
| IV | with great strain, asking again and repeats | 60-78 | 62-87 |
| V | connected text wholly unintelligible: «срыв связи» (the link fails) | < 60 | < 62 |

The standard was approved in 1972 and in force from 1 January 1974. Its listed validity ran to 1979, and whether it was
extended to 1985 is not known.

Palii puts the operator's breaking point at **about 50 % loss of information**. Voice is the hardest link to jam,
because the ear picks speech out of heavy noise in the 300-2400 Hz band ([palii_1989, USSR TT][palii-7]).

### 4.3 The ladder: weak and strong

The sources give a two-step ladder:

- **Weak jamming**: GOST class III-IV and ЩРК 3-4. Words are lost and repeats are asked for. By the manual's own rule a
  net at ЩРК 3 is already below the bar for operational traffic, so the main station may judge that a quality channel
  can no longer be kept, which is the condition for an unscheduled change of frequency (inference from §§25 and 72).
- **Strong jamming**: class V and ЩРК 1-2. The net is unusable for operational traffic: «срыв связи».

Mapping the ladder onto jam-to-signal ratios is SAM3D's own: weak from J/S 0.5 up to capture, strong at capture (J/S 2).
The weak step rests on Palii's "comparable with the signal" (inference).

## 5. Practice: what the record shows

### 5.1 The Bekaa, 9 June 1982

**The Pact's account.** A Czechoslovak General Staff colonel's analysis in the ČSLA's journal is the fullest timeline
([paprcka_1984, ČSSR ST][paprcka] pp. 50-52):

- The Israelis mapped the Syrian radar, communications, warning and air defence guidance system from mid-1981.
- Their EW grouping: **three ground radio-intelligence and jamming centres on the Golan**, able to reconnoitre and jam all
  Syrian emitters; four Boeing 707s, two C-97s (the OCR reads "C-977") and one "Harfa" for group-protection jamming; an
  E-2C as the controlling aircraft; a squadron the source calls F-4G, with Shrike and Standard ARM missiles; chaff
  balloons; and ground-launched «Zeev» ARMs with a 40 km range. "Harfa" is probably the IAI Arava, which Palii names
  (inference).
- Before dawn, about 100 aircraft came in from the sea under jamming as a deception. The radar crews stood down when the
  screens went quiet, and the alert let Israel confirm the frequencies of every Syrian radar for its ARM seekers. This is
  a single-source account.
- The main strike came in groups of 20, 30 and 50 aircraft from three directions.
- **"With the take-off of the strike aviation, intensive radio jamming began in the warning, control and cooperation
  nets"** («v sítích uvědomování, řízení a součinnosti») of the Syrian air force and ground air defence.
- **As the aircraft neared the SAM units' range**, the 707s, the C-97s and the aircraft the source calls F-4Gs began
  jamming the search and guidance radars and the air force's control radio.
- Balloons laid a chaff belt meant to drift onto the SAM firing positions by the time of the strike.
- **A few minutes before the strike the jamming was "interrupted (its intensity reduced)"** so that it would not hinder
  the anti-radiation missiles.
- 19 of 21 SAM battalions were put out of action through their guidance radars. One battalion commander who reacted in
  time and moved shot down an aircraft, moved again and shot down a second.
- Syrian fighters, their VHF radio heavily jammed from the ground and from the 707s, were not guided and returned "in
  panic".

The author blames the defeat not on the equipment but on Syrian EW practice: no continuous reconnaissance, no organised
jamming of their own, emissions not masked, radars and SAM positions not moved, and operators not trained under jamming.
The source has errors: it dates the war to July 1982 in one place, and gives Israel F-4Gs.

**The Israeli account.** The commander of the Signal Corps' EW from April 1981, Ehud Ben-Dor, joined the strike's
preparations to cut the links between the SAM batteries and their control centres ([ben_dor_2016, Israel VA][bendor]):

- "We operated four EW aircraft and blocked all their communication nets."
- The operation had never been rehearsed, and it "worked like a clock".
- **The jamming sets' batteries (power cells) overheated**, with no spares aloft; operators cooled them with wet cloths.
- The strike took off at about 13:45, and 15 of the 19 batteries were destroyed in 110 minutes (two more the next day,
  two on the last day).

These are a veteran's words 34 years on, through a newspaper. The claim that from 6 June the air force could block all
Syrian communications is the journalist's narration, not a veteran's.

**Two further accounts:**

- The IAF commander, David Ivry, said you must choose "when to jam and when not to jam": you cannot jam a net you want to
  listen to ([grant_2002, USA PR][grant]). The same article says the IAF practised "mock jamming of fighter and ground
  communications in order to undercut centralized control of the air defense". If that was pre-war rehearsal, it
  contradicts Ben-Dor; the sentence implies it without saying so. It also says selective airborne communications jamming
  cut the Syrian MiG-21s and MiG-23s off from ground control.
- The Soviet account has Arava EW aircraft, reconnaissance aircraft and the E-2C working over the sea beyond the air
  defence's reach, with ground radio-intelligence and jamming stations. Before the attack, reconnaissance fixed the radars
  and «систему радиосвязи управления силами и средствами ПВО», the air defence's control radio. 17 of 19 SAM sites were
  destroyed. **It describes radar jamming only, not the jamming of voice nets** ([palii_1989, USSR TT][palii-42];
  [p. 43][palii-43]).

The counts disagree (19 of 21, 17 of 19, 15 of 19 on the first day), and so does whether the jamming was rehearsed. The
accounts agree on the aim: to cut the batteries off from their control. For 16.2 the timing matters. Paprčka has the
voice jamming begin with the strikers' take-off, which Ben-Dor's account puts at about 13:45: **tens of minutes before
the weapons** (inference: neither source gives the minutes).

### 5.2 El Dorado Canyon, 15 April 1986

At 01:54, **six minutes before the strike**, EF-111As and EA-6Bs began active jamming of the Libyan early-warning and
SAM guidance radars. A-7Es and F/A-18s fired 48 Shrike and HARM missiles at the SAM radars, and the bombs fell at 02:00.
One of 30 attacking aircraft was lost. **Communications are not mentioned** ([palii_1989, USSR TT][palii-44]). Compass
Call's first combat was Panama in 1989 ([afmag_ec130h][afmag-ec130h]), so it was not there. No source found shows the
jamming of Libyan voice nets. The raid is a year after the game's war.

### 5.3 Angola, 1987-88

No source found shows South African jamming of FAPLA or Cuban air defence voice nets. What the sources show is
**interception**:

- The SADF's intelligence for Operation Hooper drew on artillery observers, reconnaissance regiments and "wiretapping of
  FAPLA radio networks". The paper finds no jamming ([oosthuizen_2014, South Africa ST][oost]).
- Soviet advisers knew the Angolan brigades' coded radiograms were being read, so they reported positions 5-7 km south,
  east or west of the truth. In about August 1987 the SAAF bombed exactly the reported coordinates, about 4 hours after
  the radiogram, while the brigade was 5-6 km away ([kuito_memoirs, USSR VA][kuito] pp. 89-90).
- Pechora, Kvadrat and **Osa** systems stood round the Cuito Cuanavale airfield, with a radio-technical reconnaissance
  company (p. 68). "All the brigades of the district talked on one frequency or a neighbouring one" (p. 153).
- The SAAF's 60 Squadron re-formed on 16 July 1986 with Boeing 707s and an EW role. Airframe 1421 is called "a dedicated
  COMINT and Coms Jammer platform", but when it was fitted is not known, and no source says what it jammed
  ([saaf_707, South Africa RP][saaf]).

### 5.4 Pact exercises and doctrine

An NVA colonel's article in the Warsaw Pact Unified Armed Forces staff's collection (No. 17/1979, abridged in the ČSLA's
journal in 1980) records the following ([strohschein_1980, GDR ST][stroh]):

- **Jamming was played in exercises** so that radio crews and signal units saw its effect, looked for ways to lessen it
  and gained experience. The exercises showed that radio discipline mattered even more.
- Narrowband jamming that enters a net under its stations' call signs is the most effective and the hardest to detect.

Paprčka draws these lessons ([paprcka_1984, ČSSR ST][paprcka] pp. 52-56):

- Jamming pays most at the tactical level, "where minutes and seconds decide".
- Jammers must be used by surprise, concentrated for a precisely limited time, and they will be hunted.
- Some 70-80 % of a theatre's emitters stand within 20 km of the forward edge.
- Crews and units must act automatically, because once the enemy starts jamming "a signal or command may no longer get
  through".

His figures of 30-40 air force and air defence objects per main axis and 15-20 % of the air effort for EW are what the
Pact's own front operation must destroy and allot, not the enemy's figures.

NATO believed the Pact aimed to disrupt at least 50 % of an adversary's C3 ([lawrence_1982, USA PR][lawrence]), or
50-70 % ([williamson_1980][will]), or at least 60 %, "30 percent by jamming and 30 percent by destructive fires"
([fm_24_33_1990, USA OD][fm2433-1]). The stated belief changed between 1982 and 1990.

## 6. What the jammed side did

### 6.1 The Soviet manual

The armed forces' radio manual ([rrs_1986, USSR OD][rrs]; the drill and its clock are set out in
`CREW_COMMS_2026-10.md`):

- **Keep working, and never hide the effect by switching off.** The battery regulation forbids switching a station off to
  hide the jamming's effect ([pvo_bu_ix_1984, USSR OD][bu9]).
- **Log and report.** Operators record the presence and duration of interference and the interfering stations' call
  signs, and report them; the station log records every case of deliberate interference and its nature (§71).
- **No change on duty reception.** A station on duty reception may not change frequency because of interference: it may
  only retune without radiating, to the schedule or on the signal control post's order (§68).
- **The main station orders the spare.** In a net an unscheduled change comes only by the main station's command, only
  when a quality channel can no longer be kept on the working frequencies (§§70, 72). The spare frequencies are assigned
  precisely for working under interference (§71).
- **The spare-frequency drill.** With one spare assigned, its number is not sent: the main station sends the code
  expression «Переходите на запасную частоту» on the circular call sign. The stations answer and the main station
  confirms with «Р». Then all retune at once and wait for its call, normally under new call signs. A station that hears
  no call within 1-2 minutes calls the main station; after 5 minutes stations work old and new frequencies at once
  (§§74-79; the 10-minute step is in `CREW_COMMS_2026-10.md`). Frequencies are always named by a conditional number,
  never by value. The manual's Fig. 6 example does send the spare's conditional number.
- **Say it twice.** Commands may be said twice in poor audibility and strong interference, and circular commands always
  are (§114). Each number group may be repeated digit by digit: «единица, двойка, тройка» (§112). Spoken designation, as
  bearing and range, grows longer under jamming (inference).
- **The best operators** take important traffic, and reception is duplicated where possible (§15).
- Two rules apply elsewhere, not on a battery net. The long call of up to 3 minutes is for trunk links, and the repeated
  preliminary call (up to 3 minutes) is in the radiogram rules.

### 6.2 The Pact staff's list

The Unified Armed Forces staff's article gives the drill as organisational and technical measures
([strohschein_1980, GDR ST][stroh] pp. 89-90):

- **organisational**: duplicate nets of the same purpose on scattered frequencies; hidden and reserve frequencies kept
  for extreme need; **working and reserve frequencies not close together**, so that one jammer cannot take both; changes
  of frequency on a signal, at irregular intervals, "or even after every radiogram", whether or not the enemy jams;
  important traffic on several frequencies at once or in turn; relay stations; detours through subordinates' nets that
  are not jammed;
- **technical**: directional antennas, more transmitter power, a switch from voice to telegraph, recording reception on
  tape, jam-resistant sets, and a password challenge when a station's identity is in doubt;
- radio masking: no call signs, no acknowledgements, minimum power, stations sited away from command posts, orders by
  signals and short radiograms;
- the enemy's working frequencies only with the superior staff's permission, once the main and reserve frequencies are
  silenced;
- **destroying the jammer** as the active answer.

Paprčka adds hidden numbers and frequencies, more ways and lines of command, reserve emitters, retuning or moving
against expendable jammers, and training crews under jamming ([paprcka_1984][paprcka] p. 56).

### 6.3 NATO's mirror: FM 24-33's order of remedies

The US manual of 1990 gives the other side's drill, and its order differs ([fm_24_33_1990, USA OD][fm2433-3];
[app. D][fm2433-d]):

1. Keep operating: "never shut down operations or in any other way disclose to the enemy that you are being adversely
   affected". The enemy jams, then listens for the effect.
2. Improve the signal-to-jamming ratio: retune precisely, raise power, re-orient or change the antenna, or move it "a few
   meters or several hundred meters" to put terrain between it and the jammer.
3. Relay or retransmit.
4. Last, the commander may switch the net to an alternate or spare frequency, with dummy stations left on the old one.

Appendix D adds two rules: "Do not change the frequency up or down to get away from interference", and keep radios at
least 2 km from the command post. Every suspected case is reported even if overcome.

Both sides keep working and report (inference from the three lists). The Soviet manual reaches the spare frequency
sooner, on the main station's order, once the channel's quality is lost. The US manual puts the spare frequency last.
The Pact's staff article spaces its spares and changes them irregularly.
