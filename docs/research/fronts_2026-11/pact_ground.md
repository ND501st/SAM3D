# Warsaw Pact ground doctrine as of 1985: sourced norms for the fronts (research note, November 2026)

What a Soviet-style ground force planned and expected in 1985: echelons, frontages, depths and rates; the correlation
of forces; defence; losses and when a unit stops; the march under air threat; frontal aviation's support; supply.
It is written for track F, the fronts. **The war's year is 1985.** Every number below was read in the text, with chapter
and page. Snippets are marked "snippet only". Later material is in section 5 and is never the model's number.

Three warnings about the evidence:
- The fullest period numbers come from the adversary: US Army FM 100-2-1 (16 July 1984) and FM 100-2-2 (1984).
  They are NATO's reading. Where a Soviet text gives the same norm, the Soviet text is cited first.
- The Soviet texts read are Reznichenko's *Tactics* (Voenizdat 1984, in a 1987 USAF translation) and eight articles of
  the secret *Military Thought* collection in CIA translation. Those articles were written in 1965-69 and released
  in 1973-77. They are the operator's own view, but 16-20 years before the war's year. Where FM 100-2-1 (1984) agrees
  with them, the norm is taken as still current.
- Reznichenko gives modern frontages only "according to the experience of NATO exercises" and WWII figures for his own
  side. No Soviet text read here gives a modern Soviet division frontage outright.

## 1. Summary for SAM3D's design

- **Echelons.** Against a defence in depth: two echelons, special reserves, perhaps a combined-arms reserve of about
  one-ninth of the force. Against a shallow defence: one strong echelon and a reserve. Second echelons reinforce
  success, not failure (`fm_100_2_1_1984`, ch. 2, pp. 2-10 to 2-11).
- **Objectives and depths.** Division: day's objective 25-30 km. Army: immediate 100-150 km in 3-4 days, subsequent
  250-350 km in 6-7 days. Front: final 600-800 km in 12-15 days (`fm_100_2_1_1984`, fig. p. 4-4). Volkov's 1967 front
  immediate task: 200-250 km in about four days (`mt_1967_volkov_nonnuclear`).
- **Rates of advance.** Through a defence: up to 25-30 km a day. In the operational depth: 40-50 km a day
  (`mt_kurkov_tank_army`, p. 9; FM gives 30 and 50, p. 4-4). WWII Soviet divisions managed 12-18 km a day
  (`reznichenko_1984`, p. 39).
- **Frontages.** Division attack zone 15-25 km, breakthrough on 6-10 km; regiment 3-8 km (typically 4-5); battalion
  1-2 km (`fm_100_2_1_1984`, ch. 5). Kurkov's tank army: division zone at breakthrough about 10 km, breakthrough sector
  up to 4 km (`mt_kurkov_tank_army`, pp. 5-6).
- **Correlation of forces.** An aggregate of about 3:1 in combat power suffices, counted across all organic, attached
  and supporting means (`fm_100_2_1_1984`, p. 2-11). Soviet staffs counted ratios by branch and weapon and tried
  single "coefficients of commensurability" per division (`mt_perventsev_balance`, `mt_shcherbakov_balance`).
  WWII breakthrough ratios of 6-10:1 in artillery and 4-5:1 in tanks were "hardly possible" under nuclear threat
  (`reznichenko_1984`, p. 79).
- **Defence.** Division sector 20-30 km wide and 15-20 km deep. Security zone to 30 km at army and 15 km at division.
  Regiment 10-15 by 7-10 km; battalion 3-5 by up to 2 km (`fm_100_2_1_1984`, ch. 6). Withdrawal only with the
  senior commander's permission (`reznichenko_1984`, p. 48).
- **Losses.** A large unit with losses under 40-50% counts as combat effective; above that it does not. Hits on
  command, rockets and artillery can make it ineffective at 24-25% (`mt_stashek_losses`). WWII daily losses: army
  0.6-1.2%, division 3-6%, regiment 10-16% (`mt_novoselov_replacement`, p. 4). "Combat effective status" was
  two-thirds of authorized strength (same, p. 9).
- **March.** Day 20-30 km/h, night 15-20 km/h, cross-country 5-15 km/h. 25-50 m between vehicles. Under air attack the
  column speeds up and opens to 100 m; against a large raid it leaves the road (`fm_100_2_1_1984`, ch. 5, pp. 5-2 to 5-5).
- **Aviation.** About 80% of fixed-wing aircraft go to the opening air operation, 20% plus the helicopters to the front
  (`dia_1977_fa_support`). Good penetration (0.8-0.9) needs 60-80% of the enemy's SAM fire system neutralized, costing
  about 30% of the sorties (`mt_1965_krasovskiy_conventional`, p. 8). Helicopters support only under own air defence
  cover (`fm_100_2_1_1984`, p. 12-7).
- **Supply.** First-echelon division: 2.5-3.5 units of fire on the first day of a conventional operation
  (`mt_1965_yefimov_ammunition`). POL: 12 days at front, 2-3 at army; divisions carry 3-5 days
  (`fm_100_2_2_1984`, ch. 13).

## 2. Findings

### 2.1 The offensive

- **off-01 Echelons.** Against a defence prepared in depth the Soviets attack in two echelons with special reserves and
  possibly a small combined-arms reserve. Against a defence mostly forward, one strong echelon, a combined-arms reserve
  and special reserves. The combined-arms reserve is "approximately one-ninth the size of the parent unit" when two
  echelons are used. Second echelons have missions of their own and reinforce success, not failure; they pass through
  gaps or round flanks. [`fm_100_2_1_1984`, ch. 2, pp. 2-10 to 2-11; 1984]
- **off-02 Who goes where.** Combined-arms armies normally form the front's first echelon. Tank armies go in the second
  echelon, the reserve or the OMG. A combined-arms army has 2-4 motor rifle and 1-2 tank divisions. [`fm_100_2_1_1984`,
  ch. 4, pp. 4-4 to 4-7; 1984]
- **off-03 Objectives in depth.** FM 100-2-1's figure on p. 4-4 (read from the page image): division objectives 25-30 km
  on the first day; army immediate objective 100-150 km in 3-4 days; army subsequent 250-350 km in 6-7 days; front final
  600-800 km in 12-15 days. Front offensive zone up to about 350 km. Army frontage 60-100 km. [`fm_100_2_1_1984`, ch. 4,
  pp. 4-1 to 4-4; 1984]
- **off-04 Front task, Soviet text.** Volkov (1967): the non-nuclear period might last 1-4 days in NATO exercises. The
  front's immediate task is 200-250 km deep, reached in about four days at 50-70 km a day. [`mt_1967_volkov_nonnuclear`,
  pp. 13-14; written 1967]
- **off-05 Tactical objectives.** Divisions and regiments get an immediate and a subsequent objective; battalions and
  companies an immediate objective and a direction of further advance. The p. 5-15 figure gives depth as enemy
  echelons, not km: division immediate = the enemy brigade's rear; subsequent = the enemy division's rear; regiment
  immediate = the enemy battalion's forward defences; subsequent = its rear. Against light resistance a division's final
  objective could be 80 km deep. [`fm_100_2_1_1984`, ch. 5, pp. 5-14 to 5-15; 1984]
- **off-06 WWII depths, Soviet text.** Reznichenko's table of a rifle division's missions, km from the enemy's forward
  edge: 1944 immediate 3-4, subsequent 5-7, day 10-13; 1945 immediate 3-5, subsequent 6-8, day 15-18. A NATO tactical
  defence zone is 40-50 km deep and must be broken in the first 24 h (Zapad-81). [`reznichenko_1984`, pp. 73-74; 1984]
- **off-07 Rates of advance.** FM: about 50 km a day over several weeks; fighting through defences "several kilometers
  per hour or up to 30 kilometers per day". Prepared positions are expected to be overrun in the first 2-5 days.
  [`fm_100_2_1_1984`, p. 4-4; 1984]. Kurkov: a tank army planned 40 km on day 1 and 60 km on day 2; "no more than 25 to
  30 kilometers" a day breaking through, 40-50 km a day in the operational depth. [`mt_kurkov_tank_army`, p. 9; late
  1960s]. Reznichenko: Soviet divisions in 1944-45 "did not exceed 12-18 kilometers per day"; a NATO division can now
  make 20-40. [`reznichenko_1984`, p. 39; 1984]
- **off-08 Frontages, attack.** Division zone of attack 15-25 km with three regiments up; a breakthrough with two
  regiments massed on 6-10 km. Regiment 3-8 km, "most typical" 4-5 km; 5-15 km between its echelons. Battalion frontage
  1-2 km in a 2-3 km zone, 1-3 km between echelons. Company 500-800 m. Platoon 100-200 m, 50-100 m between vehicles.
  [`fm_100_2_1_1984`, ch. 5, pp. 5-18 to 5-25; 1984]
- **off-09 Frontages, Soviet text.** Kurkov: a division's offensive zone at the breakthrough averages 10 km; a
  breakthrough sector up to 4 km for two tank regiments; zones widen to 10, 15 and 20 km after it; a tank army's zone
  is up to 100 km or more; 15-20 km gaps between divisions in depth. [`mt_kurkov_tank_army`, pp. 5-6, 9; late 1960s].
  Reznichenko gives a modern division's attack zone of 20-30 km only "according to the experience of NATO exercises",
  and WWII rifle divisions attacking on 1-2 km. [`reznichenko_1984`, pp. 39-40; 1984]
- **off-10 Approach and timing.** Division assembly area 60-75 km from the FEBA, 300-600 km². First-echelon regiments
  20-30 km back. Speed from the deployment lines to the line of attack is cut 25-50% below march speed. Regiment
  deploys into battalion columns 8-12 km out and company columns 4-6 km out. Air and artillery preparation up to
  50 min. Assault speed about 12 km/h mounted, about 6 km/h dismounted. [`fm_100_2_1_1984`, pp. 5-16 to 5-27; 1984]
  Kurkov: preparation 40-50 min with 50-60 guns and mortars per km of breakthrough sector. [`mt_kurkov_tank_army`,
  pp. 5-6]
- **off-11 Reaction and planning times.** Division 2-4 h to react, 1-3 h to plan. Regiment 1-3 h, 30 min to 2.5 h.
  Battalion 25-60 min, 20-45 min. [`fm_100_2_1_1984`, p. 5-14; 1984]
- **off-12 OMG.** A front OMG may be army-sized; an army OMG is a reinforced division, can go in on day 1 and works
  100 km or more beyond the army's other forces. It is most likely when the enemy is at low readiness or shallow.
  Division forward detachments are reinforced tank battalions; an army's forward detachment is a tank-heavy regiment.
  [`fm_100_2_1_1984`, pp. 4-5 to 4-9; 1984]. Reznichenko: forward detachments and combat helicopters can act through
  the whole tactical depth and beyond. [`reznichenko_1984`, p. 40]
- **off-13 Meeting engagement.** Regimental reconnaissance out to about 25 km, the division's reconnaissance battalion to
  about 50 km. Forward security element up to 10 km behind the combat reconnaissance patrol; the advance guard's main
  body 5-10 km behind that; the regimental commander 20-30 km behind the advance guard, which gives him about 2 hours.
  An MRR advance guard builds up: at contact 3 BTR; +20 min 10 BTR, 4 tanks, 6 mortars, 6 howitzers; +60 min 31 BTR,
  13 tanks, 6 mortars, 18 howitzers, 2 AA guns, 4 ATGMs. An hour's delay of the enemy gains 25-30 km.
  [`fm_100_2_1_1984`, pp. 5-29 to 5-34; 1984]. Reznichenko: at 25-30 km/h, forces 50-60 km apart meet in about an hour.
  [`reznichenko_1984`, meeting engagement chapter]
- **off-14 Pursuit.** The regimental commander is the lowest to order it, but all levels go into pursuit on their own.
  Tactical pursuit starts 10-20 km into the enemy's depth for a regiment, 20-30 km for a division. Division pursuit
  zone up to 30 km. [`fm_100_2_1_1984`, pp. 5-36 to 5-39; 1984]. Reznichenko: subunits pursue without waiting for
  orders; WWII rifle formations pursued 10-40 km a day, tank formations 25-80 km. [`reznichenko_1984`, pursuit section]
- **off-15 Norms and flexibility.** Norms define ideal performance. There is little tactical flexibility at battalion
  and below; the first real flexibility is at regiment. [`fm_100_2_1_1984`, ch. 2; 1984]

### 2.2 The correlation of forces

- **cof-01 Ratios, NATO's reading.** Post-war weighting of a main attack: 3-5:1 in tanks, 6-8:1 in artillery, 4-5:1 in
  personnel. Contemporary writings: an aggregate of about 3:1 in combat power suffices. It may be as small as 2:1 at the
  FEBA itself, but counting the whole force the commander may have 3:1 or 4:1. A minimum 3:1 at each level of attack.
  [`fm_100_2_1_1984`, pp. 2-10 to 2-11, 5-14; 1984]
- **cof-02 How it was reckoned.** The balance is reckoned across the whole zone to the depth of the mission, and takes in
  morale, actual strength, experience and readiness. [`fm_100_2_1_1984`, pp. 5-15 to 5-16; 1984]
- **cof-03 Coefficients, Soviet text.** Perventsev and Sokolov (1968): staffs see a set of separate ratios (they give
  an example: 1:1.1 in divisions, 2:1 in tanks, 1:3 in artillery over 100 mm) from which no overall answer follows. They
  propose a "coefficient of commensurability" per division type, derived from a computer model of a meeting engagement
  of two divisions. A Soviet MRD with R-70 missiles is 1.00. Taking communications into account, a Soviet MRD with
  R-30 is 0.90; a US mechanized division with Lance 1.29; a West German motorized infantry division with Honest John
  1.38; a British division 0.97; a Belgian mechanized division with Lance 1.20. Worked example: 18 Soviet MRDs against
  8 enemy divisions is 2.25:1 by count but 1.75:1 by quality. Tank losses come mainly from ATGMs and hand-held antitank
  weapons. Coefficients must be recomputed when equipment changes. [`mt_perventsev_balance`, pp. 4-14; 1968]
- **cof-04 How a commander used it.** Shcherbakov's review: the balance is computed by branch, by type of weapon and
  sometimes by casualty-producing capability. Which part counts most depends on the situation: nuclear means in nuclear
  war; combined-arms units, artillery and aviation in non-nuclear war; antitank means against tank counterattacks. The
  method is to compare by zone, axis and task, find strong and weak points, subtract expected mutual losses, then pick
  the grouping. Single coefficients per division were proposed and criticized. [`mt_shcherbakov_balance`; c. 1969,
  CIA 1976]
- **cof-05 WWII densities, Soviet text.** Reznichenko's table for the main axis by period of the war (first, second,
  third): artillery 20-60, 120-200, 200-250 guns and mortars per km; tanks 8-15, 15-20, 20-30 per km; rifle battalions
  1.5-2, 3-4, 5-7 per km; ratios artillery 1.5-2, 4-6, 6-10:1; tanks 0.6-1.5, 2-3, 4-5:1; infantry 1.5-2, 3-4, 3-5:1.
  Breakthroughs needed "200 or more guns and 30 to 40 tanks per kilometer". Today it "would hardly be possible" to mass
  the same densities, though the need for decisive superiority stays; superiority is sought more in quality.
  [`reznichenko_1984`, pp. 79-80; 1984]

### 2.3 Defence

- **def-01 Why defend.** Among the reasons: to regroup after severe losses, to await logistic support, to hold ground
  while forces mass elsewhere. Hasty defence is expected to be more common than prepared defence. The operational
  reserve and second echelon may be half the force. [`fm_100_2_1_1984`, ch. 6, p. 6-1; 1984]
- **def-02 Security zone.** Up to 30 km deep at army and 15 km at division. When established before contact, the
  security echelon is up to 15 km forward of the main defensive area and is drawn from the division's second echelon.
  It withdraws under artillery cover when faced with encirclement or decisive engagement. [`fm_100_2_1_1984`,
  pp. 6-2 to 6-5; 1984]
- **def-03 Division.** A tank or motor rifle division "typically defends a sector 20 to 30 kilometers in width and 15 to
  20 kilometers in depth", in two echelons and a reserve, at least two regiments forward. In an MRD a tank regiment is
  the main counterattack force. Forward CP as close as 3 km. [`fm_100_2_1_1984`, p. 6-5; 1984]. Reznichenko, from NATO
  exercises: a division defends 20-35 km wide and over 20 km deep; a WWII rifle division 8-10 by 6-8 km.
  [`reznichenko_1984`, pp. 39-40]
- **def-04 Regiment, battalion, company.** Regiment 10-15 km wide, 7-10 km deep, dispersed so one low-yield strike takes
  out no more than a company. Battalion 3-5 km wide, up to 2 km deep, up to 2 km between echelons. Company strongpoint
  500-1000 m wide, up to 500 m deep. Fire lines 400-600 m apart; antitank fire out to 3 km; 100-200 m between tanks.
  [`fm_100_2_1_1984`, pp. 6-7 to 6-9; 1984]
- **def-05 Counterattack.** Needs the next higher commander's authority. Delivered from the flank, from the march,
  tanks leading. A mounted reserve moves at 20-30 km/h by day, 15-20 km/h at night. [`fm_100_2_1_1984`, pp. 6-3,
  6-8; 1984]
- **def-06 Withdrawal.** Reznichenko: withdrawal is done to remove forces from a strike, gain time or take a better
  line, and "only with the permission of the senior commander (chief)". [`reznichenko_1984`, p. 48; 1984]. FM: a
  covering force (a reinforced platoon per forward company), a rearguard (normally a reinforced second-echelon
  battalion), then the main body; rear services leave first; at night; minimum radio. [`fm_100_2_1_1984`,
  pp. 6-10 to 6-11; 1984]
- **def-07 Relief in place.** "Units being relieved have usually sustained considerable losses." At night, platoon by
  platoon. [`fm_100_2_1_1984`, pp. 6-11 to 6-12; 1984]
- **def-08 Positional and mobile defence.** No period text read here defines a Soviet mobile defence (подвижная
  оборона) as current 1985 doctrine. FM 100-2-1 describes a defence in depth with fire sacks, counterattacks and a
  security echelon, not a mobile defence. Open item 7.4.

### 2.4 Losses, combat effectiveness and reconstitution

- **loss-01 The threshold.** Stashek: "if they are under 40 to 50 percent, the large unit is considered combat
  effective; if they are greater, it is not". But research showed two divisions with 24% and 25% losses lost
  effectiveness because their command, rocket and artillery means were hit. He asks for a qualitative assessment
  first. [`mt_stashek_losses`, p. 7; c. 1968]
- **loss-02 Effective strength.** Novoselov: the replacement calculation keeps battalions and artillery subunits "in
  combat effective status (two-thirds of authorized strength)". [`mt_novoselov_replacement`, p. 9; c. 1969]
- **loss-03 Daily losses, WWII.** "Average daily losses in any [an] army ran 0.6 to 1.2 percent; in divisions 3 to 6 percent;
  and in regiments 10 to 16 percent". In the operational depth they were 2-3 times lower than in a breakthrough. The
  heaviest losses came in long, slow operations with little superiority. [`mt_novoselov_replacement`, p. 4; c. 1969]
- **loss-04 Losses in a nuclear operation.** Estimates of 30-60% for an army operation (40-60% on the main axes). By
  arm: divisions 10-80%, rocket brigades 40-90%, air defence 20-90%, artillery up to 50%. An army losing 40-50% needs up
  to 10,000 men of reserve components. One or two reserve divisions per first-echelon army. Units that lose
  effectiveness are withdrawn and re-formed. Mobilized reserves need at least 3-5 days to move 800-1200 km.
  [`mt_novoselov_replacement`, pp. 5-10; c. 1969]
- **loss-05 Restoring effectiveness.** Stashek's exercise: more than half the front's large units lost 30-50% to the
  enemy's nuclear strike; about 30% of first-echelon units could act within 3-4 h. Restoring an army's control took about
  1 h through a forward CP; otherwise 2-3 h up to days (4-6 h by helicopter, 10-12 h by vehicle, 7-9 h through a
  division staff). The air army lost up to 20%; only 15% of the survivors could act at once. [`mt_stashek_losses`;
  c. 1968]. Reznichenko: losses used to grow slowly, mostly in first-echelon infantry and tanks; now heavy losses may
  come at once through the depth. Restoration is: restore control, update the missions of units still effective,
  withdraw struck subunits to restore them, replace men and equipment, usually in the old structure, sometimes as
  combined units. [`reznichenko_1984`, pp. 54-55; 1984]
- **loss-06 Replacement, NATO's reading.** Individual, incremental, composite units (up to division or corps), and
  whole units: "entire armies" brought forward to replace first-echelon forces rendered ineffective.
  [`fm_100_2_2_1984`, ch. 13, pp. 13-22 to 13-23; 1984]
- **loss-07 Damage criteria.** Destruction: 90% probability that at least 50% of the subelements or area are seriously
  damaged. Suppression: 25-30%. [`fm_100_2_1_1984`, ch. 8, p. 8-1; 1984]
- **loss-08 What fraction ends an attack.** No text read gives a loss fraction at which an attack is called off. The
  doctrine instead commits the second echelon or reserve where the first is stopped, and only to success (off-01).
  The nearest numbers are loss-01 and loss-02. Open item 7.3.

### 2.5 The march, dispersal and air threat

- **march-01 Routes.** Up to four routes per division, at least 3 km apart; a march zone up to 25 km wide.
  [`fm_100_2_1_1984`, ch. 5, p. 5-1; 1984]
- **march-02 Speeds.** Mixed columns: day on roads 20-30 km/h, night on roads 15-20 km/h, cross-country 5-15 km/h.
  [`fm_100_2_1_1984`, p. 5-2; 1984]. Reznichenko: average speeds of 25-30 km/h are now attainable; 20 km/h in mountains
  or forest; on foot 4-5 km/h; several hundred km a day is the norm. WWII night marches 15-20 km/h, day 20-25 km/h.
  [`reznichenko_1984`, march chapter; 1984]
- **march-03 Road surface.** Vehicle speeds by road damage, km/h: concrete or asphalt 40-50 undamaged, 20-35 with 10% of
  the surface destroyed; gravel 40-45 / 20-30 / 10-20; dirt 15-25 / 8-15 / 5-10 (the third column, heavier damage, is
  blank for concrete in my reading). [`fm_100_2_1_1984`, p. 5-3; 1984]
- **march-04 Intervals.** Vehicles in a company 25-50 m; companies in a battalion 25-50 m; battalions on one route
  3-5 km; regiments 5-10 km; regimental rear services 3-5 km behind; division rear services 15-20 km behind. Under
  nuclear conditions vehicle intervals rise to 300 m or more. [`fm_100_2_1_1984`, pp. 5-2 to 5-3; 1984]. Reznichenko
  agrees on 25-50 m. [`reznichenko_1984`, march chapter]
- **march-05 Halts.** Short halts 20-30 min every 2-3 h, at least 10 m between vehicles. Long halts of 2-4 h on marches
  over 24 h. [`fm_100_2_1_1984`, p. 5-4; 1984]
- **march-06 Air attack on the march.** The column "speeds up and vehicle spacings are increased to a distance of up to
  100 meters". If a large group of aircraft attacks, the column may disperse or seek concealment off the road.
  [`fm_100_2_1_1984`, pp. 5-5 to 5-10; 1984]
- **march-07 Assembly areas.** Under NBC threat 50 m between vehicles and companies at least 1.5 km apart.
  [`fm_100_2_1_1984`, ch. 16, pp. 16-11 to 16-12; 1984]. Division assembly 300-600 km² (off-10).

### 2.6 Frontal aviation's support

- **air-01 The air operation first.** A non-nuclear front offensive begins with an air operation of several days; the
  Soviets accept great air losses. A small share of aircraft neutralizes air defence and opens corridors; most strike
  nuclear systems, C2 and airfields. In the first hours fixed-wing aircraft give no direct support and the ground
  forces rely on helicopters. [`fm_100_2_1_1984`, ch. 4 and ch. 12, pp. 12-1 to 12-2; 1984]. DIA 1977: about 80% of
  fixed-wing aircraft (1,465) to the air operation, 20% (365) plus all assault helicopters to the front commander; the
  front's share grows as the war goes on; ground-force air defence is the primary counterair force over the troops.
  [`dia_1977_fa_support`; 1977]
- **air-02 Four stages.** Support of the movement forward, preparation, support, accompaniment. Preparation reaches no
  farther than the immediate operational depth (the enemy corps' rear) and lasts 10 min to over an hour. Accompaniment
  begins when the troops outrun the fire plan. [`fm_100_2_1_1984`, pp. 12-1, 12-8; 1984]
- **air-03 Depths.** Interdiction up to 480 km behind the front line. Exercise safety distances from own troops 200-700
  m. Fixed-wing reconnaissance beyond 400 km. [`fm_100_2_1_1984`, pp. 12-4 to 12-5; 1984]. Krasovskiy (1965):
  fighter-bombers and bombers strike at low level to 200-400 km, at high level a further 200-500 km.
  [`mt_1965_krasovskiy_conventional`, p. 7; 1965]
- **air-04 What decides crossing the enemy's air defence.** Krasovskiy: the most favourable conditions are a
  probability of 0.8-0.9 of getting through the enemy air defence. That needs many groups at once "and with 60 to 80
  percent neutralization of the fire system of enemy surface-to-air missiles", costing about 30% of the forces planned.
  Up to 30% of the front's fighter-bombers and bombers stay on nuclear alert, so about 70% are free.
  [`mt_1965_krasovskiy_conventional`, p. 8; 1965]. Volkov (1967): about 50% of fighter-bombers and bombers, 25-30% of
  the air army, held for nuclear use. [`mt_1967_volkov_nonnuclear`]
- **air-05 Shares of effort.** Krasovskiy: at least 50-60% of fighter-bomber effort to support of the ground troops; up to
  25% of fighter-bomber and 60% of bomber effort against nuclear means. If the troops' preparatory fire neutralizes
  40-45% of important targets, the share drops to 20-25% during support. An air army destroys about 30-40% of the
  targets missed by ground fire in 3-5 days. [`mt_1965_krasovskiy_conventional`, later pages; 1965]
- **air-06 Helicopters.** Preferred for immediate strikes close to own troops. Because of fighters the Soviets "prefer to
  employ them in ground support only to the range of their air defense umbrella"; fixed-wing aircraft go deeper.
  3-6 s to identify the target, clear within 10 s, approach at 50-100 m. They attack after the artillery preparation,
  or through corridors. Near the FEBA they "depend on local air superiority". [`fm_100_2_1_1984`, pp. 12-7 to 12-8 and
  ch. 7; 1984]. Reznichenko: combat helicopters can act through the whole tactical depth (off-12). [`reznichenko_1984`,
  p. 40]

### 2.7 Supply norms (brief)

- **sup-01 Planning lots.** Units of fire, refills of fuel and daily rations. The basic load is a multiple of the unit of
  fire, set before each operation. [`fm_100_2_2_1984`, ch. 13, pp. 13-11 to 13-13; 1984]
- **sup-02 Ammunition, Soviet text.** Division mobile reserves 1.0 unit of fire; a mobile army artillery depot 0.25. In a
  conventional operation a first-echelon division may need 2.5-3.5 units of fire on the first day alone, when as much
  as half of an enemy infantry division is in its zone. In nuclear war:
  1.3-2.0 on day 1, then 0.2-1.0 a day (0.8-1.2 against counterstrikes or in a meeting engagement, 0.2-0.5 in pursuit).
  Army: 2.0-2.8 on day 1, 0.3-0.4 a day after, 3.5-5.2 for a 6-7 day operation. WWII: 0.03-0.25 a day.
  [`mt_1965_yefimov_ammunition`; 1965]
- **sup-03 Fuel and rations.** Front POL depots 12 days, army 2-3 days. Advance bases when army depots are over 100 km

## 4. Disagreements and which to use

- **Rate of advance in the depth.** FM 50 km/day; Kurkov 40-50; Volkov 50-70 for the front's immediate task. Through
  defences FM and Kurkov agree (30 vs 25-30). Use 25-30 km/day breaking through and 40-50 km/day in depth; 50-70 only
  against a broken enemy.
- **The combat-effective threshold.** Stashek: under 40-50% losses still effective. Novoselov: effective status is 2/3 of
  authorized strength (a third lost). These are two different tests: Novoselov's is the replacement target, Stashek's
  the limit of use. Use both: above 2/3 full, between 1/2 and 2/3 degraded and first to be replaced, below 1/2 (or a
  lost HQ at about 25%) ineffective and withdrawn.
- **Force ratio.** FM's 3:1 aggregate against Reznichenko's WWII 6-10:1 artillery and 4-5:1 tanks, which he says are no
  longer possible. Use 3:1 by weighted strength over the axis, with 3-5:1 locally on a breakthrough sector.
- **Division frontage.** FM 15-25 km (three regiments up); Kurkov about 10 km at the breakthrough for a tank division;
  Reznichenko's 20-30 km is a NATO division in NATO exercises. Use 15-25 for a division's zone and 6-10 for its
  breakthrough.
- **Helicopters' reach.** FM: only under own AD. Reznichenko: through the whole tactical depth. Use FM's rule for
  support near the line and allow raids into the tactical depth only through corridors made by SEAD.
- **Aircraft held for nuclear use.** Krasovskiy up to 30%; Volkov about 50% of fighter-bombers and bombers (25-30% of the
  air army). In a conventional 1985 war use Krasovskiy's 30% as the reserve.
