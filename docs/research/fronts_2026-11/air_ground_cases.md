# The air front over the land battle: when air forces moved forward, what stopped them, how fast air defence came back (research note, November 2026)

Track F ("the fronts") gives the enemy air force an *air front*: by default at the border, moved forward in a sector
only when the enemy believes our air defence there is broken and enough of our mobile ground assets are destroyed, so
the risk is low and the reward high. This note collects the period evidence for that behaviour, mostly for low-level
attack aircraft and helicopters, and for the other half of the loop: how fast air defence coverage was rebuilt or moved.

**The period is 1965-85; the war's year is 1985.** Afghanistan after the Stinger (late 1986) and anything from 1991 on
is hindsight (section 8). Source ids are the ones in `docs/SOURCES.md`, or new ones in section 10.

This note extends `docs/research/air_battle_2026-11/factions_cases.md` and does not repeat it. Already covered there:
the Bekaa strike of 9-10 June 1982 as a SEAD model (nato-10), Dugman 5 (nato-11), Vietnam jettisons and Bolo (nato-12),
Syrian and Iraqi pilots (pact-12), Crippa, Biafra's MiniCOIN, Katanga's Fouga, the SAAF's husbanding of its F1s in
1987, Austria's Goldhaube and Sweden's STRIL 60. This note uses those only as cross-references.

Four warnings about the evidence:
- Most numbers come from the attacker's side (US studies of Israel, CIA and NPIC photo-interpretation of Arab SAM
  sites). Arab and Soviet participants' numbers are rare. The Soviet *Military Thought* articles are the exception.
- "Site", "battery" and "battalion" are not the same unit. NPIC and FRUS count *sites* (prepared positions, occupied or
  not); Werrell, Brant and Lambeth count *batteries*. Do not add them up.
- No source states the air front rule as a number. The thresholds in section 7 are SAM3D estimates, bounded by cases.
- Several cases are one-off, and some secondary accounts disagree by a factor of two. Each disagreement is recorded.

## 1. Summary for SAM3D's design

**What the period says, in eight lines.**
1. **Air forces did not fly close support over an intact, dense, mobile SAM belt for long.** The IAF tried on 6-8
   October 1973, lost perhaps 50 aircraft in three days at the Suez front, and for the moment stopped flying within
   10-15 miles (16-24 km) of the canal (`werrell_2005`, p. 151).
2. **They came back over that ground as soon as the belt had a hole.** On 16 October 1973 Israeli ground forces crossed
   the canal; by midday four SAM sites were destroyed and by the next morning the IAF was "operating in full support"
   of the ground forces (`werrell_2005`, p. 153). The army had cleared a SAM-free corridor some 15 miles wide (`brant_1986_bai_1973`, ch. 4).
3. **The trigger was local, not theatre-wide.** In 1973 the hole was a corridor on the west bank, not the whole belt.
   In 1982 the IAF re-rolled aircraft held for SAM suppression to close support once the early SAM strikes went better
   than expected (`nwc_83_027`, pp. 20-21).
4. **A desperate ground situation overrode the losses.** On the Golan the IAF lost about 80 aircraft in 19 days and
   Israeli leaders judged that preferable to not flying a maximum effort (`entwistle_1988`, ch. 3-5). Brant: the IAF
   was the only reserve that could stop the crossing, so it abandoned its normal policy of clearing air defences first.
5. **Helicopters stayed on their own side unless the air was theirs.** Soviet doctrine as the US read it: helicopters
   near the FEBA depend on local air superiority (`fm_100_2_1_1984`, p. 7-1). US doctrine of 1977: unless suppressed,
   air defences force attack helicopters to fight "in the ground environment in forward areas" (`fm_17_50_1977`,
   p. 1-1). When Egypt sent helicopters across the line against an intact defence it lost 20-35 of about 50 Mi-8s on
   6 October 1973 and all five in a raid on 18 October (`werrell_2005`, p. 153).
6. **Defenders rebuilt fast when the attacker let them, and slowly when he did not.** Unopposed, Egypt went from 15-25
   to 58-61 probably operational SAM sites in the canal standstill zone in about eight weeks (`frus_1969_76_v23_d179`,
   p. 615). Under attack in 1982, Syrian batteries replenished overnight were destroyed the next day (`lambeth_1984`,
   p. 8). New SA-6 units came within about three weeks; SA-5 complexes took six to eight months (section 4).
7. **Prepared but empty positions were the norm.** In spring 1973 NPIC counted 254 unoccupied SAM sites and 206
   showing dummy equipment against about 124 occupied (`npic_1973_sam_egypt`). A mobile defence moves between prepared
   positions; the attacker's picture of "where the SAMs are" goes stale.
8. **"Broken" was measured in shares, but no one wrote down a threshold for the air front.** Soviet norms call a target
   suppressed at 25-30% of its elements seriously damaged and destroyed at 50% (`fm_100_2_1_1984`, p. 8-1). US joint
   SEAD doctrine of 1982 suppresses "to levels commensurate with" the objectives and plans follow-on campaigns as the
   enemy reconstitutes (`jsead_1982`, pp. 3, 16).

**What this means for track F.** The air front should advance per sector, on the enemy's *believed* picture, when the
believed working fire units covering a strip of the sector fall below a share and leave a corridor of a usable width;
helicopters advance one step later than fixed-wing low-level aircraft; the front pulls back on a loss rate, not on a
single loss; and our side's rebuild time is a property of prepared positions and what the enemy lets us do. Section 7
gives the numbers.

## 2. Moving forward over ground the enemy's air defence had covered (question 1)

### 2.1 The War of Attrition, 1969-70: the air front pushed the SAMs back, then the SAMs pushed the air front back

- **f-01 Israel destroyed the first SAM line along the canal in 1969.** Werrell: in September-October 1969 the IAF
  "took out" the Egyptian SAMs along the canal; in January 1970, with new US ECM pods, it neutralised the Egyptian air
  defence within three months by destroying three-quarters of its early warning radars (`werrell_2005`, p. 147). Brant
  gives a single eight-hour raid on 25 December that destroyed every missile battery from Qantara to Suez City
  (`brant_1986_bai_1973`, ch. 4). The two accounts differ on dates; both show the IAF clearing the strip first and then
  flying deep and against artillery.
- **f-02 Then it kept its air front at the canal.** After Israeli pilots met Soviet-flown MiG-21s south of Cairo on
  18 April 1970, the IAF adopted "a new policy of flying only in the air space around the Suez Canal" (Brant, ch. 4).
  This is a political air front: set by who defends the rear, not by SAM attrition.
- **f-03 The SAMs walked back to the canal under fire.** By the end of June 1970 Egypt had 55 SAM batteries; SAMs
  downed three IAF aircraft in one week; the IAF destroyed five batteries in response (`werrell_2005`, pp. 147-148).
  FRUS records "a big Soviet and Egyptian push to move SAMs toward the Suez Canal" in the weeks before the standstill
  of 7 August 1970 (`frus_1969_76_v23_d179`, p. 615). NPIC counted 93 SA-2 and 18 SA-3 sites in Egypt by mid-1970,
  up 18 and 10 since March (`npic_1970_sam_egypt_syria`).
- **f-04 Losses in the war of attrition were low for the attacker.** Werrell: the IAF lost 15 aircraft (13 to ground
  fire) between July 1967 and January 1970, and 27 (25 to ground fire) over the whole war of attrition as he dates it
  (`werrell_2005`, pp. 147-148). The SAM line moved forward only when the attacker stopped striking it.

### 2.2 The Suez front, October 1973: under the umbrella, then over the hole

- **f-05 The umbrella.** About 60 SAM sites stood along the canal in a strip 160 km long and 20 km deep, with about 100
  more spread through Egypt (`brant_1986_bai_1973`, ch. 4). Werrell counts 150 Egyptian batteries, 46 of them SA-6,
  and nearly 47 Syrian batteries, 32-35 of them SA-6 (`werrell_2005`, p. 148). NPIC's last pre-war count (photography
  of March-May 1973) was 66 occupied SA-2 sites, 48 SA-3, about 10 SA-6, 206 sites with dummy equipment and 254
  unoccupied (`npic_1973_sam_egypt`; the SA-6 figure is hard to read in the OCR).
- **f-06 Egypt's own air force stayed under it.** Bergquist: the Arab plan kept the armies inside the air defence
  umbrella and held the air forces back as a reserve, to step into breaches or exploit a weakened Israeli air force
  (`bergquist_1988`, pp. 11-13). Brant: between 9 and 13 October the EAF flew only limited low-level attacks "by small
  numbers of aircraft which remained behind the air defense umbrella" (ch. 4). When Egyptian armour left the umbrella on
  14 October it was beaten (`werrell_2005`, p. 152; Bergquist quotes Shazly to the same effect, p. 13).
- **f-07 Egypt sent its air force forward only when the crossing was threatened.** From 16 to 19 October the EAF
  attacked the Israeli crossing sites in waves of jets and helicopters "despite crippling losses", and on the 19th the
  Supreme Command committed most of the air reserve (`brant_1986_bai_1973`, ch. 4). This is the defender's air force
  going *forward* out of desperation, the mirror of f-11.
- **f-08 The ground force opened the hole.** On 16 October Israeli forces crossed the canal. By midday four SAM sites
  were destroyed and by the next morning the IAF was in full support of the ground forces; "in reverse of the accepted
  practice, the army made it possible for the air force to operate" (`werrell_2005`, p. 153). One account credits an
  armoured division with 34 batteries, about half the canal SAMs. By the ceasefire of 22 October about 40 of the 55-60
  batteries Egypt had in action were destroyed, by aircraft and ground forces together (same page).
- **f-09 How the IAF used the hole.** The forces on the west bank created a SAM-free corridor about 15 miles wide.
  Israeli attack aircraft came in very low over Sinai, climbed in the cleared airspace, and dived on the perimeter of
  the bridgehead; "for the first time" ground commanders could count on the IAF clearing positions ahead of the
  advance (`brant_1986_bai_1973`, ch. 4). Brant's conclusion: the umbrella was not fully effective over the west bank
  because Israeli ground forces had neutralised many of the batteries (ch. 5).
- **f-10 The air front moved within a day of the first sites going.** From the first SAM site destroyed (midday 16 Oct)
  to full close support (morning 17 Oct) is under 24 hours (`werrell_2005`, p. 153). The IAF did not wait for the whole
  belt to fall: about 40 of 55-60 batteries were destroyed only by 22 October.

### 2.3 The Golan, October 1973

- **f-11 Maximum effort into intact defences.** Syria committed all available aircraft to make up for less SAM and AAA
  coverage than Egypt had (`entwistle_1988`, ch. 4). The IAF lost about 80 aircraft at the Golan in 19 days: over 50%
  to AAA, under 10% in the air, one or two to SA-7s, and an estimated 30 (40%) to the SA-6, about 7% of all IAF
  fighters (`entwistle_1988`, ch. 4). Israeli leaders judged high losses preferable to not flying a maximum BAI effort
  (ch. 5). The IAF's first days there were therefore *not* a risk-low, reward-high choice; they were forced.
- **f-12 The Golan belt broke faster than the Suez one.** Concentrating first on Syria, the IAF destroyed half the
  Syrian SAMs in four days; Syria also ran out of SA-6 missiles; one source adds a destroyed control centre
  (`werrell_2005`, p. 152). Syrian ground forces that attacked outside their umbrella lost protection (`brant_1986_bai_1973`, ch. 4).
- **f-13 Helicopters at Mount Hermon.** On 21 October Israeli paratroopers were lifted onto Mount Hermon under fighter
  cover, with a diversionary raid on Damascus. The Syrian heliborne counter-attack lost six escorting fighters and three
  troop-laden helicopters (`brant_1986_bai_1973`, ch. 4). Helicopters crossed the line only where the side using them
  held the air.

### 2.4 Lebanon, June 1982: suppression first, then close support

- **f-14 Close support followed air superiority.** "Israeli CAS operations were conducted efficiently, effectively and
  with relatively light opposition following the achievement of air superiority" (`nwc_83_027`, p. 20). Aircraft first
  earmarked for SAM suppression were re-rolled to CAS when the early SAM strikes went better than planned; later even
  F-15 and F-16 CAPs strafed ground positions (pp. 20-21).
- **f-15 The order was planned before the war.** After 1973, IAF planners were told that close support and interdiction
  came after air superiority and defence suppression, and that the IAF would most likely not be available for close
  support in the early stages of a future war (`nwc_83_027`, pp. 59-60). This is the rule the air front encodes.
- **f-16 Israeli helicopters went forward with the ground battle.** The IDF repeatedly outflanked Syrian defenders in
  the Bekaa with helicopter-lifted tank-killer teams (`nwc_83_027`, p. 54). The CIA's 1984 lessons paper expected
  Israeli helicopters to take over more of the close support role, freeing jets for other ground attack (`cia_1984_lebanon_lessons`, para. 81).
- **f-17 Syrian helicopters went forward too, without air superiority.** Syrian SA-342 Gazelles with HOT missiles
  attacked Israeli armour from 8 June. Only tier 4 accounts were found: the Jewish Virtual Library says 14 Syrian
  Gazelles were shot down and captured over Lebanon in 1982 (`jvl_gazelle`); claims of tank kills differ widely and are
  not used. Pattern only: a weaker side may push helicopters forward in its own defence while its air force stays back.
- **f-18 Helicopters were also the trigger for the SAMs.** The CIA dates the Syrian SA-6 move into the Bekaa (April
  1981) to Israeli aircraft downing two Syrian troop-carrying helicopters near Zahlah; the deployment was preplanned
  (`cia_1984_lebanon_lessons`, Discussion, footnote on the "red lines"). Werrell gives 19 batteries moved in late April
  1981 (`werrell_2005`, p. 154).

### 2.5 Iran-Iraq, 1980-85

- **f-19 Neither air force flew true close support over the front.** Iranian planners judged from the Arab-Israeli and
  India-Pakistan wars that CAS against a sophisticated air defence was too costly. Iranian fighters attacked behind the
  zone of contact, along the roads to the battle, not at it (`bergquist_1988`, p. 59). Iraq likewise seems to have
  dropped CAS rather than face its own trigger-happy gunners and Iran's defences (same page).
- **f-20 Both gave the job to helicopters, and the helicopters went where the enemy had no air cover.** Attack
  helicopters were first reported in early October 1980. Iranian TOW Cobras had considerable success against Iraqi
  armour that advanced without air cover; in mid-October 1980 three Cobras took turns on a column near Abadan and
  apparently faced no anti-aircraft fire. By November Iraqi ZSU-23-4s and tank machine guns began to take a toll
  (`bergquist_1988`, pp. 59-60). Helicopters stayed in use to the end, "regularly but not in large numbers", and both
  air forces backed away from ground support as losses mounted (p. 61).
- **f-21 Iraq's air force, as the CIA saw it, flew high and avoided losses** (`cia_1987_iraq_af`, already in
  `factions_cases.md` pact-12): the order not to lose aircraft was used as an excuse. That is a loss-averse air front.

## 3. What stopped them (question 2)

- **f-22 A loss rate, not a single loss.** At the Suez front the IAF lost dozens of aircraft, "perhaps as many as 50",
  in three days, twice the rate of 1967; the Israelis "for the moment, stopped flying within 10-15 miles of the Suez
  Canal" but kept flying on the Syrian front where the situation was grave. In the first week the IAF lost 78-90
  aircraft (`werrell_2005`, p. 151). The registered JVL figure is 50 aircraft in about 1,220 sorties in the first three
  days, about 4% per sortie (`jvl_1973_losses`, tier 4). Over the whole war IAF losses were "just over 1 percent" per
  sortie against 4% in 1967 (`werrell_2005`, p. 154); Brant gives one aircraft per hundred sorties (ch. 4).
- **f-23 What the losses bought.** Brant: forced low by the SA-2 and SA-3, IAF aircraft flew into the ZSU-23-4, SA-6
  and SA-7; targets attacked by air were limited to one pass, and the ground support promised "did not come close to
  what was requested" (ch. 4). Werrell: one study found aircraft did not unequivocally damage or destroy one tank in
  the first three days (p. 154). High risk and low reward: the two conditions of the air front, both visible.
- **f-24 Political and escalation limits.** Flying only round the canal after meeting Soviet pilots (f-02). In 1982-83
  the Soviet-manned SA-5 sites in Syria were judged a "powerful restraining factor" on Israeli planning (`lambeth_1984`,
  p. 14), and the US embassy in Tel Aviv reported the cabinet "exceedingly wary" of pre-empting them
  (`state_1982_sa5_cable`). A neutral's or a superpower's crews in the defence push the air front back without a shot.
- **f-25 The rule for helicopters.**
  - *Soviet, as the US read it (1984):* helicopters flying near the FEBA depend on local air superiority; when it is
    denied they are used to put down observation posts and patrols rather than to reconnoitre from the air
    (`fm_100_2_1_1984`, p. 7-1). In the initial hours of the air operation, while jets attack theatre targets, ground
    commanders rely on combat helicopters for direct support, and helicopters help open corridors through the enemy's
    forward air defences (p. 12-1). Helicopters may support forward detachments beyond artillery range in a pursuit
    (p. 12-9).
  - *Soviet, in a 1985 US study:* attack helicopters stage from airfields or sites well behind the FEBA "and definitely
    beyond enemy artillery range", wait in loiter positions just out of enemy artillery range, and attack with a pop-up
    at 3-5 km. Airmobile assaults go 20-30 km beyond the FEBA, with Hinds as escort (`barbeau_1985_sams`, pp. 14, 23-24).
  - *US (1977):* unless suppressed, long-range air defences force attack helicopters to fight in the ground environment
    in forward areas; a deliberate attack on a well-prepared position needs "considerable suppression and careful
    mission profile planning"; field and air defence artillery and USAF fighter-bombers suppress so that attack
    helicopters can move about (`fm_17_50_1977`, pp. 1-1, 3-3, 3-13). Exploitation deep into the enemy rear begins only
    when the enemy "is obviously unable to continue maintaining an organized defensive system" (p. 4-30).
  - *What happened when the rule was broken:* Egypt's heliborne commandos on 6 October 1973, 20-35 of about 50 Mi-8s
    downed; the attack on the canal bridge on 18 October, all five helicopters downed (`werrell_2005`, p. 153); the
    Syrian heliborne counter-attack on Mount Hermon, three helicopters (f-13).
- **f-26 The defender's own air force was stopped by its own SAMs.** Arab SAMs destroyed 45-58 Arab aircraft in 1973,
  more than Israeli aircraft (39-44); Syrian SAMs downed 20 Iraqi MiGs on 8 October (`werrell_2005`, p. 153). For
  SAM3D: our side's aircraft do not fly freely inside our own belt either (cross-reference `our_side_air.md`).

- **f-27** The first weeks were "modifications and improvements in previously existing facilities"; a surge of
  construction came between late August and mid-September; occupying already-built sites continued after new building
  slowed (`frus_1969_76_v23_d179`, p. 615). **Coverage roughly tripled in eight weeks without attack, mostly by
  occupying prepared sites.** Israeli figures at the time (40-50 batteries in the zone) are press claims and not used.

### 4.2 Egypt and Syria, 1973: many more sites than units

- **f-28** NPIC's spring 1973 count: about 124 occupied sites (66 SA-2, 48 SA-3, about 10 SA-6), 206 with dummy
  equipment, 254 unoccupied, 2 under construction. Four new SA-6 sites within 17 nm of the canal were occupied on 18
  and 31 March and empty on 1 and 10 May (`npic_1973_sam_egypt`). **About two prepared positions per unit, and SA-6
  units shuffled between them on a scale of weeks.**
- **f-29** Syria after 1973 "almost tripled" its ground-based air defences, from 30 to 80 batteries, by 1981
  (`werrell_2005`, p. 154). That is a peacetime rebuild of eight years, not a wartime norm.

  **about three weeks** for new mobile SA-6 units into the area; **six to eight weeks** for short-range SA-8s; **six to

### 4.4 North Vietnam, 1965-67: moving after every firing

- **f-32** Soviet analysis of the DRV's air defence (`mt_1973_nvn`, pp. 10-11): SAM battalions changed launch sites
  after every firing, by night. A move of 30-40 km took 10-15 hours: 2.5 hours to pack up, 3 hours to set up, and
  2-2.5 hours to check and adjust the equipment. Radar companies had alternate positions 15-20 km away and moved in one
  night. Old positions were turned into dummies with dummy launchers and imitation launches. The cost: when battalions
  had moved ahead of raids, Hanoi and Haiphong were at times left uncovered, and the command revised the movement tactics.
- **f-33** The US side: in the first nine months of 1966 US aircraft flew 75 strikes against 60 sites and claimed 25
  destroyed and 25 damaged; such attacks proved unprofitable because of the defences and because the SAMs "could be
  relocated within hours" (`werrell_2005`, pp. 120-121).

### 4.5 Soviet norms for the redeployment of a mobile battery

- **f-34** Divisional SA-6 and SA-8 batteries relocate as needed, "leaving at least one battery in firing position to
  cover for the one(s) moving" (`fm_100_2_1_1984`, p. 11-9). The SA-6 is deployed 3-6 miles behind the leading elements,
  five batteries "three up and two back", TEL reload about 10 minutes (`entwistle_1988`, appendix 3). Between attacks,
  regimental air defence weapons are repositioned to survive and to deceive (`fm_100_2_1_1984`, p. 11-8).
- **f-35** For the Osa itself SAM3D already holds the norms: march to combat position at most 4 minutes (`nvo_2015`),
  3 minutes in Russian norms (`wrk`), 6.8 minutes from cold (`flak11`); see `docs/research/OSA_RESEARCH_2026-10.md` §3.
  Not re-read here.

## 5. The rebels' and the neutral's appetite (question 4)

### 5.1 Small and insurgent air arms near a front

- **f-36 The Falklands, 1982: a small air arm's ground attack achieved little.** The US Navy's assessment: Argentina
  used its helicopters "almost entirely" for logistics, utility support and surveillance; Canberras and Pucaras flew
  in the attack role "apparently without effect" (`usn_falklands_1983`, sections on aviation). Werrell records one
  British Scout helicopter downed by a Pucara (`werrell_2005`, p. 161). Pattern: a small force's helicopters stay in
  the rear; its light attack aircraft go forward but rarely tell.
- **f-37 Angola, 1985: a small, embargoed air force went forward only when its client was about to fall.** The CIA:
  the Angolan offensive of 1985 nearly took Mavinga "until driven back by apparently repeated South African airstrikes";
  South Africa then cut back its air build-up in northern Namibia, suggesting Pretoria believed the danger to UNITA had
  passed (`cia_1985_angola_offensive`). In May 1986 the CIA noted that the Angolans had not moved their mobile SAMs to
  cover troops in the field, making them "an easy target", and that the South African forward airfields had no early
  warning radar nets or long-range SAMs (`cia_1986_angola_saaf`). Pattern: the small air force's front stays back and
  surges forward for days when (a) the reward is decisive and (b) the enemy's troops have no air cover.
- **f-38 Nicaragua, 1983-86: rebels without air defences cannot hold ground.** The CIA: helicopters (40 Mi-8, Mi-17
  and Mi-25) and forward airbases and landing zones near the guerrilla areas improved the government's close support;
  "as long as they lack adequate air defenses, the rebels will be constrained" from moving beyond small-unit actions to
  seize and hold towns (`cia_1986_nicaragua_af`). This is the reverse case: the government's air front advances freely
  over rebels with no air defence.
- **f-39 Biafra and Katanga** (already in `factions_cases.md`, rebels-01/02): night and tree-top raids by a handful of
  aircraft against airfields and the UN. No case found of an insurgent air arm moving a sustained "front" forward; their
  raids were point attacks.

### 5.2 A neutral defending its own airspace

- **f-40 Austria and Switzerland, 1978.** An Austrian and a Swiss staff officer at the US CGSC described both
  countries' concept as *dissuasion*: to make entry into the country so expensive and time-consuming that an aggressor
  would choose other options (`sadauer_schlup_1978`, pp. 29-35). Austria's air force: air surveillance and
  interception, air defence, close support, reconnaissance and transport; the aggressor was expected to try first to
  destroy Austria's air force and surveillance system (pp. 47, 54). Switzerland's air force and anti-aircraft troops:
  warning, protection of neutrality in the air, defence of Swiss airspace, harassment of enemy forces, reconnaissance
  and close support; "the main mission of the air force remains the fighting of ground targets", but their employment
  "must not lead to an untimely attrition" of units needed for the real defence (pp. 88-90).
- **f-41 Reading for SAM3D.** A neutral's air front is its own border, by doctrine. It supports its army inside its own
  territory and spends aircraft slowly. The Noric Republic's `max_depth_km` of 40 (60 after an event) and loss tolerance
  of one a day fit this; a forward move of its air front beyond the border should need an event (an invasion of its
  territory, a political decision), not just a broken defence.
- **f-42 Not found:** Swedish or Finnish period doctrine in a tier 1-2 text (only the tier 4 pointers of
  `swe_stril_c2` exist). Open item.

## 6. Measuring "coverage" and "degradation" in the period (question 5)

- **f-43 Soviet fire norms (as the US read them, 1984).** An area target is *suppressed* when there is a 90%
  probability that at least 25-30% of its elements or area are seriously damaged, and *destroyed* at 50%
  (`fm_100_2_1_1984`, p. 8-1 and table p. 9-24). These are artillery norms, but they are the Soviet language of
  "suppressed" a Pact planner would apply to an enemy air defence grouping.
- **f-44 Soviet air planning: suppression as a share of the force.** Bobrov (*Military Thought* No. 10, 1984): in
  local wars after Korea, support groups made up 50-60% of the aircraft in a raid, reconnoitring, jamming and attacking
  the air defences; in Korea a strike on the power system put up to 35% of 500 aircraft into an echelon suppressing
  AAA; NATO's "Cold Fire" exercises, as the Soviets read them, had a first echelon suppressing the air defences in a zone
  up to 120 km wide, with strike groups following through the corridors it opened (`mt_1984_bobrov`). Babich: the attacks
  of the strike and suppression groups are timed so that the enemy cannot restore the combat capability of his air
  defence (`mt_1984_babich`). FM 100-2-1: in the air operation a small part of the force neutralises air defences and
  opens approach corridors (p. 12-1).
- **f-45 US joint SEAD, 1982.** The J-SEAD text has *campaign* SEAD (theatre-wide, against long-range SAMs and early
  warning radars well behind the lines, planned before the war and flown in its first days, with follow-on campaigns
  "depending upon ... the enemy's ability to reconstitute") and *localized* SEAD (against short-range and medium SAMs
  where aircraft work, including corridors for transiting the FLOT) (`jsead_1982`, pp. 16-17, 20). Destructive
  suppression is cumulative; disruptive means sustain it "once the threats have been reduced to levels commensurate with
  the J-SEAD objectives" (p. 3). The text gives no percentage threshold.
- **f-46 Israel measured success by batteries destroyed in the target area.** 17 of 19 SA-6 batteries in the Bekaa on
  day one (`lambeth_1984`, pp. 6-8; `werrell_2005`, p. 155): about 90%. The IAF then moved to close support (f-14). Before
  the war the Bekaa complex was 19 sites, 40-60 SA-6 TELs with 180 missiles, 12 SA-2 launchers and 7 SA-3 launchers,
  covered by 450-500 AAA pieces (`nwc_83_027`, p. 16).
- **f-47 SAM3D's existing gate.** NATO's rollback phase in `faction_nato.json` ends when intelligence believes fewer
  than 45% of the air defences work (`until_iads: 0.45`). The cases above sit both sides of it: the Suez hole opened at
  well under 30% destroyed theatre-wide but near 100% locally; the Bekaa near 90% locally. **The period's decision was
  local.** A sector gate should be local too.

## 8. Post-period items (hindsight, not used as the model's numbers)

- Afghanistan after the Stinger (from late 1986): Soviet aircraft moved to high altitude and helicopters changed
  routes and heights (`markovsky_afghan_sky`, `rgs_afghan_2002`). Not used for 1985.
- The 1991 Gulf War: the Iraqi air defence collapsing under a theatre SEAD campaign, kill boxes, A-10s pushed to medium
  altitude after losses. Not used.
- 1999 Kosovo: the Serbian mobile SAMs surviving 78 days by moving and keeping silent. A strong case for "the picture
  goes stale", but after the period.
- `bergquist_1988` (December 1988) and the CIA's 1986 papers on Angola and Nicaragua were written after 1985. They are
  used only for events of 1980-85.
- Angola 1987-88 (Cuito Cuanavale, the SAAF's F1 losses) is after the war's year (already flagged in `factions_cases.md`).
- Werrell (2005) and Entwistle (1988) were written later but are histories of period events; their numbers describe
  1966-82.
- `fm_1_112` as found on archive.org (labelled 1981) mentions systems of the 1990s; it is not the period edition and
  was not used.
