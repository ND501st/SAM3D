# NATO ground and air-ground doctrine as of 1985: sourced norms for the fronts (research note, November 2026)

What the US Army and NATO planned and expected in 1985 on the ground and in the air over it: the layered battlefield
(covering force, main battle area, rear area, deep battle), the lines that divide it, how air support is split by
depth, losses and when a unit stops, rates of advance, the march under air threat, and how NATO's own air defence moved
with the battle. It is written for track F, the fronts, where NATO is the enemy invading a small Pact-aligned country.
**The war's year is 1985.** Every number below was read in the text, with chapter and page. Snippets are marked
"snippet only". Later material is in section 5 and is never the model's number.

Four warnings about the evidence:
- **NATO's 1985 doctrine is defensive.** It was written to stop a Pact attack in Germany ("forward defence"). A NATO
  invasion of a small country is the game's fiction. The attack norms below come from the offence chapters of the same
  manuals and from history. The defence norms describe what NATO would do when our side counterattacks.
- **The period US manuals give kilometres for the enemy, not for themselves.** FM 100-5 (1976 and 1982), FM 71-100
  (1978), FM 71-2 (1977) and FM 71-3 (1980) give Soviet frontages and depths in km, but US sectors only as "METT-T"
  (mission, enemy, terrain, troops, time). The 1982 manual measures depth in hours, not km. US frontages in section 3
  are therefore SAM3D estimates, bounded by what the texts do say.
- **FM 100-5 of 1982 dropped the numerical force ratios** of the 1976 edition. The 3:1 and 6:1 figures are 1976
  doctrine, superseded by 1985 but still the rule of thumb the planners and the planning tables used.
- **Dupuy and HERO are analysis, not doctrine.** Their advance rates and breakpoints are historical statistics from
  WWII, labelled here as analysis (tier 4).

## 1. Summary for SAM3D's design

- **The battlefield in layers.** In defence a corps fights five elements: a deep battle forward of the FLOT, a covering
  force, the main effort in the main battle area (MBA), rear area protection and a reserve (`fm_100_5_1982`, p. 11-2).
  The covering force is "normally established by corps" and stands far enough forward to stop the enemy's
  medium-range artillery reaching the FEBA (p. 11-5). Its typical tasks are told in time: "72 hours, for seven days,
  or until some action has been completed" (`fm_100_5_1976`, p. 5-12).
- **Depth in hours (1982).** Area of influence: battalion 3 h, brigade 12 h, division 24 h, corps 72 h, echelons
  above corps 96 h. Area of interest: 12, 24, 72, 96 h and beyond (`fm_100_5_1982`, p. 6-2). Corps watches for 96 hours'
  notice of approaching enemy divisions and armies; divisions fight enemy forces up to 24 h before they reach the FLOT
  (p. 7-15).
- **Depth in km.** 1976: captains see 0-5 km, colonels 0-50 km, generals 0-150 km forward of the FEBA
  (`fm_100_5_1976`, p. 7-12). 1982: division and corps sensors cover out to 150 km beyond the FLOT (`fm_100_5_1982`,
  p. 6-4). In FOFA planning 150 km is "the limit of the NATO corps' area of responsibility" (`ota_1987_fofa`, ch. 5,
  pp. 79-80).
- **Force ratios (1976).** A defender should beat an attacker of "about 3:1"; the attacker should seek "at least 6:1 at
  the point of decision" (`fm_100_5_1976`, p. 3-4). Generals must never be "outnumbered or outgunned more than 3:1"
  in defence and should concentrate "about 6:1" to attack (p. 3-5). With heavy air and artillery on good ground a
  defence may hold at "something like 5:1 for short periods" (p. 5-3).
- **Air support by depth.** CAS near own troops, needing detailed integration; BAI against targets "positioned to
  directly affect friendly forces", planned jointly, on either side of the FSCL inside the ground commander's area of
  influence; AI deeper, with no detailed integration (`fm_101_5_1_1980`, pp. 1-3, 1-15, 1-16; `fm_100_5_1982`, p.
  7-11). In 1975 DePuy and Dixon split interests by range: the Army leads within 5 km, the Air Force beyond 50 km,
  5-50 km is shared (`herbert_1988_lp16`, pp. 70-71).
- **The FSCL** is set by corps (or an independent division) on terrain seen from the air (`fm_101_5_1_1980`, p.
  1-49). FM 6-20 wanted it "as close to the forward elements as possible" (`fm_6_20_1977`, p. D-5). FOFA planning put it
  at "about 30 kilometers" (`ota_1987_fofa`, ch. 5, pp. 78-79).
- **FOFA** (approved by NATO's Defence Planning Committee on 9 November 1984): category 1, regiments, 5-30 km;
  category 2, divisions, 30-80 km; category 3, divisions, 80-150 km; category 4, armies, 150 km to about 17° E;
  category 5, fronts, to the Soviet border (`ota_1987_fofa`, ch. 3 and 5). SACEUR expected fresh Pact formations at the
  FEBA "about 36 hours" after an offensive began (`rogers_1983_fofa`).
- **Losses for planning.** A division in contact loses 1.0-6.6% of its strength a day, by type of action
  (`fm_101_10_1_1976`, table 5-8, p. 5-8). Tanks: a covering force heavily committed in delay loses 73% of its
  operational tanks on day 1; a division defending the MBA heavily loses 54%; up to 80% of the losses are reparable
  (table 7-7, p. 7-16).
- **No period doctrine fixes a "combat-ineffective" percentage.** Army officers' folklore was 20-30% (30% most
  credited); a declassified 1954 study called a fixed figure "a gross oversimplification not supported by combat
  data" (`clark_1954_oro`). HERO: battles end when one side gives up its mission, almost never when it cannot fight on
  (`hero_1982_termination`).
- **Advance rates (analysis).** Normalised WWII rates: 2.8 km/day against intense resistance, 10.1 against moderate,
  23.3 against slight, 54.8 against negligible, about 70 unopposed (`hero_1972_oralfore`, p. IV-2). FM 100-5 (1982)
  plans corps and divisions on operations "that may cover 50 to 80 kilometers daily" (p. 8-7).
- **The march under air threat.** Open column 50-100 m between vehicles (15, 12, 10 vehicles per km); close column
  about 25 m (about 30 per km); a mechanised task force is 6 km long in close column and 18 km in 100 m open column;
  infiltration is the best passive defence (`fm_71_2_1977`, p. H-4). Convoys move at 15-20 mph (`fm44_3_1981`, p. 6-21).
- **NATO's own SAMs move with the battle.** Direct-support Hawk batteries "leapfrog forward keeping advancing battalion
  task forces under their coverage"; Chaparral cannot fire on the move and is set at critical points
  (`fm44_3_1981`, pp. 7-5, 6-22). No period source gives a rebuild time for lost Hawk or Chaparral units; the one
  period voice says a lost system "can not be replaced" in a short war (`curran_1985_ada`).

**What this means for track F.**
- Model NATO's front as a corps-sized layered belt: a covering force 1-3 days of delay deep, an MBA, a rear area, and a
  deep battle out to 150 km driven by corps.
- Model NATO's air front by depth bands tied to the FLOT: CAS to the FSCL, BAI either side of it inside 72 hours'
  march, AI beyond. Move the FSCL by corps order on terrain lines; push it forward in a fast advance.
- Let NATO's air front move forward where our air defence is broken: J-SEAD zones split the work by depth (Army to the
  limit of observed fire, the Air Force beyond: `davis_1987_31_initiatives`, p. 31), so a broken belt lets CAS and BAI
  work deeper at once, and helicopters cross the FLOT only behind SEAD.

## 2. Findings

### 2.1 Active defence (1976) against AirLand Battle (1982)

- **gnd-01 The 1976 manual counts.** "The defending force should be able to defeat an attacker superior in combat power
  by a ratio of about 3:1." The attacker "should seek a combat power ratio of at least 6:1 at the point of decision"
  (`fm_100_5_1976`, ch. 3, p. 3-4, page image checked). Generals deploy "so that on the defense they are never
  outnumbered or outgunned more than 3:1 at the point and time of decision, and so that in the attack they have
  concentrated combat power of about 6:1 superiority" (p. 3-5). [1976]
- **gnd-02 The 1976 concentration rule.** Soviet doctrine puts up to six divisions in depth on a 10-12 km front:
  20-25 battalions and up to 600 tanks in the first echelons (`fm_100_5_1976`, p. 3-5). Division commanders "must be
  willing to concentrate firepower and up to six to eight of their maneuver battalions on one-fifth of their front to
  meet breakthrough forces of 20-25 battalions". They cover the rest with cavalry, remaining battalions and attack
  helicopters. If that is not possible, they "trade space for time by going to the delay" (p. 5-3, page image
  checked). Defenders "should seek not to be outweighed more than 3:1"; with very heavy air and artillery on favourable
  terrain, "something like 5:1 for short periods" (p. 5-3). [1976]
- **gnd-03 The 1976 covering force.** Four tasks: make the enemy reveal his main attack, deceive him, strip his air
  defence umbrella (make him displace it before he attacks the MBA), and gain time. Normally armoured and air cavalry,
  reinforced; a reinforced armoured cavalry regiment as a corps covering force, or a division's cavalry squadron. It may
  be told to fight "for 72 hours, for seven days, or until some action has been completed by the main body"
  (`fm_100_5_1976`, ch. 5, pp. 5-10 to 5-12). [1976]
- **gnd-04 1976 counterattack.** Counterattacks only "when the gains to be achieved are worth the risks involved in
  surrendering the innate advantages of the defender" (`fm_100_5_1976`, p. 5-14). [1976]
- **gnd-05 The 1982 manual drops the numbers.** "Force ratios and the effects of fire and maneuver are significant in
  deciding battles; however, a number of intangible factors often predominate" (`fm_100_5_1982`, p. 2-4). Herbert's
  history says the 1976 rule had turned the principle of mass into the procedural "not less than 6:1 at the point of
  decision" (`herbert_1988_lp16`, p. 101). [1982]
- **gnd-06 The 1982 defence has five elements.** Deep battle in the area of influence forward of the FLOT; a covering
  force "normally established by corps"; the main effort in the MBA, "either at the FEBA or further to the rear"; rear
  area protection; a reserve "best used to strike a decisive blow" (`fm_100_5_1982`, p. 11-2). [1982]
- **gnd-07 The 1982 covering force.** It stands "far enough forward of the FEBA to protect MBA units from surprise,
  allowing MBA commanders to reposition forces ... and preventing enemy medium-range artillery fire on the FEBA". It may
  be the first echelon of a two-echelon defence. Normally tank-heavy task forces and cavalry; ideally one or more
  armoured cavalry regiments per corps. "Light covering forces will be able only to harass the enemy and to report his
  movement" (`fm_100_5_1982`, p. 11-5). FM 71-100 adds: the covering force area runs from the line of contact back to
  the FEBA; in front of a division it "could well consist of up to four or five tank heavy battalion task forces or
  cavalry squadrons"; the time it must hold "must be stated" (`fm_71_100_1978`, pp. 5-12 to 5-13). [1978-82]
- **gnd-08 Depth in hours, 1982.** Areas of influence (time beyond the FLOT or attack objectives): battalion up to 3 h,
  brigade 12 h, division 24 h, corps 72 h, echelons above corps 96 h. Areas of interest: 12, 24, 72, 96 h and beyond
  96 h (`fm_100_5_1982`, p. 6-2). The corps area of influence "should extend far enough beyond the FLOT to permit a
  corps to engage enemy forces which can join or support the main battle within 72 hours"; divisions "should be able
  to fight enemy forces up to 24 hours before they reach the FLOT" (p. 7-15). [1982]
- **gnd-09 The deep battle.** "Battlefield air interdiction ... is the most common means of striking at extended
  ranges" (`fm_100_5_1982`, p. 7-15). Division and corps reconnaissance cover out to 150 km beyond the FLOT; beyond
  that, echelons above corps (p. 6-4). [1982]
- **gnd-10 Offence, 1982.** The corps or division "must coordinate and support all arms and control operations that
  may cover 50 to 80 kilometers daily and change direction frequently" (`fm_100_5_1982`, p. 8-7). Night attacks on
  strong positions "should cover relatively short distances (1 to 2 kilometers from the line of departure to the
  objective)" (p. 9-16). [1982]
- **gnd-11 Delay, 1982.** Delays "gain time by forcing the enemy to concentrate repeatedly against successive battle
  positions". A delaying commander may defend first and shift to delay "only after the enemy has concentrated
  overwhelming combat power"; to hold forward of a line until a time he must "accept a decisive engagement". Cavalry
  "should execute the delay" (`fm_100_5_1982`, ch. 12, pp. 12-1 to 12-2). [1982]
- **gnd-12 The ground is shallow.** From the inner-German border: Hamburg under 40 km, Frankfurt about 100 km, the
  Rhine-Ruhr under 150 km; 30% of West Germany's population within 100 km of the border (`rippe_1985_airland`, ch. 4,
  quoting period sources). NATO's defence was forward because it had no room. [1985]
- **gnd-13 Frontages of own units: not given.** None of FM 100-5 (1976, 1982), FM 71-100 (1978), FM 71-2 (1977) or
  FM 71-3 (1980) gives a US corps, division, brigade or battalion frontage in km. They give Soviet ones (for example a
  Soviet battalion on about 1,000 m, narrowing to 700-800 m at the breakthrough: `fm_71_2_1977`, ch. 2; a Soviet
  division defending 20-30 km wide and about 15 km deep: `fm_71_3_1980`, ch. 4, not re-read). The US frontages in
  section 3 are SAM3D estimates.

### 2.2 The control measures

- **ctl-01 Definitions (US, 1980).** FLOT: "a line which indicates the most forward positions of friendly forces in
  any kind of military operation at a specific time" (`fm_101_5_1_1980`, p. 1-53). FEBA: "the forward limit of the main
  battle area" (p. 1-52). Line of contact: "a general trace delineating the location where two opposing forces are
  engaged" (p. 1-62 area; entry read). [1980]
- **ctl-02 The FSCL.** "A line, normally placed on terrain identifiable from the air, beyond which all targets may be
  attacked by any weapon system (including aircraft and special weapons) without endangering friendly troops or
  requiring additional coordination with the establishing headquarters so long as the effects of the weapon do not fall
  short of this line." "It is normally established by corps or independent division" (`fm_101_5_1_1980`, p. 1-49). The
  coordinated fire line (CFL) is for surface fires only, "usually established by brigade or division" (p. 1-36 area;
  entry read). [1980]
- **ctl-03 Where the FSCL goes.** FM 6-20: it "should be established as close to the forward elements as possible
  consistent with the tactical situation"; "CAS is normally planned short of the FSCL and air interdiction is planned
  beyond", but "the FSCL is not a clear-cut dividing line" (`fm_6_20_1977`, app. D, p. D-5). In a fluid attack the fire
  support coordinator "must recommend that the FSCL be kept well forward" (ch. 4); a worked example puts the corps FSCL
  "3 km beyond Line PICK" (p. 4-19). [1977-80]
- **ctl-04 The FSCL in FOFA planning.** FOFA category 1 attacks "would begin at the region of the FSCL, about 30
  kilometers" (`ota_1987_fofa`, ch. 5, pp. 78-79; OCR damaged, the 30 km reading is clear in two places). Jauron's
  history says FM 6-20 (1977) no longer required placing the FSCL close to friendly troops, and made CAS within 5 km of
  the front need a forward air controller (`jauron_1993_fscl`, pp. 8-9; post-period, not checked in FM 6-20's text).
  [1977-87]
- **ctl-05 Who moves them.** The FLOT and line of contact are facts on the ground. The FEBA and the covering force
  area are drawn by the commander who orders the defence (division or corps) (`fm_71_100_1978`, p. 5-12). The FSCL
  moves by corps order; the CFL by brigade or division (`fm_101_5_1_1980`). The 1982 manual: an area of interest "will
  change with the forward or rearward movement of the FLOT" (`fm_100_5_1982`, p. 7-15).

### 2.3 Air-ground: CAS, BAI, AI, J-SAK, FOFA, the 31 Initiatives, attack helicopters

- **ag-01 The three kinds of air support (US terms).** CAS: "air attacks against hostile targets which are in the
  proximity of friendly forces and which require detailed integration of each air mission with the fire and maneuver of
  those forces" (`fm_101_5_1_1980`, p. 1-16 area). BAI: "air action against hostile ground targets which are positioned
  to directly affect friendly forces and which require joint allocation, planning, and coordination"; it "may not
  require continuous coordination during the execution stage" (p. 1-15). AI: conducted "at such distance from friendly
  forces that detailed integration of each air mission with the fire and movement of friendly forces is not required"
  (p. 1-3). [1980]
- **ag-02 BAI in AirLand Battle.** BAI "is the primary means of fighting the deep battle at extended ranges"; it
  "destroys, delays, or disrupts follow-on enemy units before they can enter the close battle". "BAI missions may be
  planned against targets on either side of the FSCL in the ground commander's area of influence. Missions short of the
  FSCL require close coordination with ground units." CAS needs aircraft "under either positive or procedural
  control"; the ground commander must suppress enemy air defences for it (`fm_100_5_1982`, p. 7-11). [1982]
- **ag-03 ATP-27(B) and the agreements.** NATO's ATP-27(B), *Offensive Air Support* (1979), made BAI a new kind of
  offensive air support: aircraft attack reinforcements and lines of communication "in the immediate rear of the enemy
  frontline", distinct from interdiction "at distances beyond the battlefield"; once requested, BAI was "entirely under
  the Air Component Commander's direction". The US Air Force and Army accepted it in a May 1981 agreement on offensive
  air support; it became both services' doctrine on 28 November 1984 (`davis_1987_31_initiatives`, pp. 30-31). ATP-27(B)
  itself was not found; only these descriptions. [1979-84]
- **ag-04 J-SEAD by depth.** The TAC-TRADOC-USREDCOM concept of 3 April 1981 gave the Army "primary responsibility for
  J-SEAD to the limits of observed ground fire" and the Air Force from there "to the limits of Army unobserved indirect
  fire. Beyond that, zone suppression was entirely an Air Force responsibility." The air component commander set a
  "preferred suppression sequence" by system type, which the Army could modify for its own aviation
  (`davis_1987_31_initiatives`, p. 31; see also `jsead_1982`). [1981-82]
- **ag-05 J-SAK.** By 1979 TAC and TRADOC were discussing the "Joint Attack on the Second Echelon"
  (`davis_1987_31_initiatives`, p. 30). "In late 1982 the services signed the Joint Operational Concept Joint Attack of
  the Second Echelon (J-SAK)" (`ota_1987_fofa`, ch. 3). Initiative 21 (BAI) led to TAC and TRADOC's "General Operating
  Procedures for Joint Attack of the Second Echelon" in December 1984, tested in Blue Flag 85-3 six months later and
  judged adequate in November 1985 (`davis_1987_31_initiatives`, p. 76). [1982-85]
- **ag-06 The 31 Initiatives.** Signed by the two chiefs of staff on 22 May 1984. Those that touch track F: 1 area SAMs
  and air defence fighters, 2 point air defence, 3 counter heliborne assault, 10 rear area CAS, 15 J-SEAD, 21 BAI,
  24 CAS, 25 air liaison officers and FACs, 27 Joint STARS (`davis_1987_31_initiatives`, p. 48). [1984]
- **ag-07 FOFA.** SHAPE studied the attack of follow-on forces from 1982; the Defence Planning Committee approved
  SACEUR's Long Term Planning Guideline for FOFA on 9 November 1984 (`ota_1987_fofa`, ch. 3). Depth bands (ch. 5, pp.
  78-80; ch. 6, p. 83):

  | Category | Target | Depth beyond the FLOT | Aim |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | 1 | second-echelon regiments | beyond direct fire (about 5 km) to the FSCL region (about 30 km) | destroy |
  | 2 | second-echelon divisions | about 30 to 80 km | destroy ("kill" the regiment) |
  | 3 | follow-on divisions | about 80 to 150 km ("the limit of the NATO corps' area of responsibility") | disrupt, delay |
  | 4 | follow-on armies | 150 km to about 17° E (300-400 km east of the inner-German border) | disrupt, delay; bridges on the Oder and Neisse |
  | 5 | follow-on fronts | 17° E to the Soviet border (600-850 km) | delay; rail transloading at the gauge change |

  In category 1 a regiment's columns are on the road for only 30-60 minutes; the whole regiment moves in 1.5-2.1 h; a
  corps facing a main attack may see seven such regiments moving in a day, over 9 hours (IDA, quoted in
  `ota_1987_fofa`, p. 83). [1984-87]
- **ag-08 SACEUR's FOFA aims.** The Pact could bring "fresh formations at the FEBA about 36 hours after its offensive
  has begun and additional fresh forces at similar intervals". Targets: railways (power, switching, loading sites,
  river crossings), road choke points, logistic sites, communications, assembly areas. Striking deep "will keep the
  force ratios at the FEBA manageable" (`rogers_1983_fofa`). [1983]
- **ag-09 The 1975 split by range.** DePuy (TRADOC) and Dixon (TAC): the Army has the stronger voice within 5 km of the
  front ("the maximum range of Army direct-fire weapons"), the Air Force beyond 50 km, 5-50 km "a mutual concern"
  (`herbert_1988_lp16`, pp. 70-71). [1975]
- **ag-10 The airborne FAC stands back.** Because of the density of enemy air defences, "the airborne FAC is still
  necessary but must operate at low level and at a safe distance from the FEBA, perhaps as far as 15 kilometers back",
  handing off to a ground FAC or forward observer (`fm_100_5_1976`, p. 8-5). [1976]
- **ag-11 Attack helicopters, 1977 doctrine.** "Enemy weapons that can interfere ... must be suppressed. Field and air
  defense artillery and USAF tactical fighter bombers should be employed to provide sufficient suppression"
  (`fm_17_50_1977`, ch. 3 introduction). Contour flight forward of the division rear boundary; nap-of-the-earth forward
  of the rear boundary of a committed brigade (ch. 4, p. 4-11). The forward area refuel and rearm point is best 17-25 km
  from the battle position; helicopters rearm 2-7 times a day (p. 6-3). The 1977 manual is about fighting over own
  ground and in exploitation; it gives no cross-FLOT depth. [1977]
- **ag-12 Attack helicopters, 1986.** Under AirLand Battle the attack helicopter battalion's "top priority" is the
  close-in reaction to massed armour, "followed by the beyond FLOT operation". At the National Training Center in August
  1986 attack helicopters "produced very few kills and suffered several losses", which was "not an unusual outcome"
  (`sharpe_1986_ah`, pp. 1, 21). [1986, just after the war's year]

### 2.4 Losses and combat effectiveness

- **loss-01 Daily personnel losses, % of strength (division in contact; battle + nonbattle = total).**
  `fm_101_10_1_1976`, table 5-8, p. 5-8 (page image checked). Use for periods of up to 5 days. Divisions in corps reserve
  lose about 0.6% a day total; nondivisional corps units 0.4-0.8%.

  | Action | Battle | Nonbattle | Total |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Covering and security force action | 0.9 | 0.3 | 1.2 |
  | Attack: meeting engagement | 2.4 | 0.3 | 2.7 |
  | Attack of a position, day 1 / later days | 3.8 / 1.9 | 0.3 | 4.1 / 2.2 |
  | Attack of a fortified zone, day 1 / later days | 6.3 / 3.2 | 0.3 | 6.6 / 3.5 |
  | Defence: meeting engagement | 1.5 | 0.3 | 1.8 |
  | Defence of a position, day 1 / later days | 1.9 / 1.0 | 0.3 | 2.2 / 1.3 |
  | Defence of a sector, day 1 / later days | 3.2 / 1.6 | 0.3 | 3.5 / 1.9 |
  | Inactive (in contact, neither side attacking) | 0.7 | 0.3 | 1.0 |
  | Pursuit | 1.3 | 0.3 | 1.6 |
  | Retirement and delaying action | 0.7 | 0.3 | 1.0 |

  The manual says the factors rest "primarily on World War II and Korean war experience and will not necessarily
  apply during another war" (p. 5-7). Battle losses by branch: armoured division 62% infantry, 23.1% armour, 3.6%
  artillery (table 5-9). [1976]
- **loss-02 Tank losses, % of operational tanks (armoured or mechanised division).** `fm_101_10_1_1976`, table 7-7,
  p. 7-16 (page image checked). Days 2 and 3 apply to the tanks on hand at the start of day 1.

  | Day | Delay (covering force area) light | moderate | heavy | Defend (MBA) moderate | heavy |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | 1 | 12% | 30% | 73% | 22% | 54% |
  | 2 | 13% | 18% | 8% | 12% | 14% |
  | 3 | 6% | 11% | 6% | 6% | 10% |

  Assumptions: a conventional European war, "a threat to friendly force ratio of three or four to one". A tank loss is
  anything that stops it fighting; "up to 80 percent of the losses are reparable" by organisational and direct-support
  maintenance, averaging 10 man-hours. Worked example: a covering force of 30-50% of the division's manoeuvre units,
  heavily committed, loses 70-75% of its tanks on day 1; the division, then "moderately committed", loses 30%. [1976]
- **loss-03 The 20-30% folklore.** "Estimates of Army officers usually fall in the range of 20 to 30 percent, and 30
  percent has been given wide credence." Clark's study of 44 infantry battalions in seven WWII engagements found that a
  fixed percentage is "a gross oversimplification not supported by combat data"; ranges, not averages, must be used, and
  they differ for a breakpoint "from attack to defense" and one "from defense to withdrawal or collapse"; officer losses
  run proportionately higher at breakpoints; leadership, fire support and reinforcement explain much of the spread
  (`clark_1954_oro`, summary and purpose). [1954, declassified; analysis]
- **loss-04 Battles end on the mission, not on exhaustion.** "Battles almost never last until one side is unable to
  continue fighting." The failing commander "will conserve his force for future battles"; "if the mission is judged
  very important or essential, very high casualties will be accepted" (`hero_1982_termination`, essay on battle
  endings). [1982; analysis]
- **loss-05 What was found for "combat-ineffective".** No period US or NATO doctrine read here sets a strength
  percentage below which a unit is combat-ineffective. FM 101-10-1 plans losses, not breakpoints. AR 220-1 readiness
  ratings exist but are peacetime readiness, not battle effectiveness (not read; open item).
- **loss-06 Dupuy's posture factors (analysis).** The QJM's "military posture" values: attack 1.0, hasty defence 1.3,
  prepared defence 1.5, fortified defence 1.6, withdrawal 1.15, delay 1.2 (Dupuy, *Numbers, Predictions and War*, 1985
  edition, p. 48, as reproduced in `ciano_1988_qjm`, fig. 24; the 1979 first edition was not seen). These are combat
  power multipliers in Dupuy's model. They say a prepared defence is worth 1.5 times its strength, a long way below the
  1976 manual's 3:1. [1979-85; analysis]

### 2.5 Rates of advance

- **adv-01 History in the 1976 manual.** WWI divisions advanced 2-4 km a day. Guderian's XIX Corps averaged 30.5 km a
  day to the Channel (best day 90 km); Rommel's 7th Panzer Division 22 km a day; Guderian's 2nd Panzer Group 28 km a day
  over 24 days in Russia (best day 120 km) (`fm_100_5_1976`, pp. 2-31 to 2-32). [1976]
- **adv-02 Planning, 1982.** Corps and division operations "may cover 50 to 80 kilometers daily" (`fm_100_5_1982`,
  p. 8-7). This is the pace of an exploitation, not of a breakthrough. [1982]
- **adv-03 HERO's normalised WWII rates.** Six WWII operations (German 1940-42, US 1944), normalised for terrain,
  weather, roads and opposition (`hero_1972_oralfore`, pp. III-8 to III-9 and IV-2; page image checked):

  | Opposition | Overall range, km/day | Average | Corps average | Armoured division average |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | Intense resistance | 1.32-4.04 | 2.80 | 2.76 | 2.89 |

### 2.6 Dispersal against air attack

- **disp-01 March columns.** Close column: vehicles "approximately 25 meters apart during daylight", about 30 vehicles
  per km, "little dispersion", normally at night. Open column: 50-100 m or more, normally by day; 15 vehicles per km at
  50 m, 12 at 75 m, 10 at 100 m. A mechanised infantry-heavy task force is 6 km long in close column and 18 km long in
  100 m open column. Infiltration (single vehicles or small groups at irregular intervals) "provides the best possible
  passive defense against enemy observation and attack" (`fm_71_2_1977`, app. H, p. H-4). [1977]
- **disp-02 March control.** "A two-minute interval between march units is fairly standard." Messengers are the
  primary means; radio "only in emergencies". Every vehicle has an air guard; some vehicles orient wholly on air
  observation (`fm_71_2_1977`, pp. H-5 to H-7). Worked example: speed 24 km/h, rate of march 20 km/h (p. H-10).
  [1977]
- **disp-03 Assembly areas.** Overhead concealment, good exits and internal routes, "space for dispersion", a helicopter
  landing site; before an attack "out of range of enemy light artillery" if possible; units leave the road "without
  slowing or halting" (`fm_71_2_1977`, pp. H-7 to H-8). [1977]
- **disp-04 Convoys and the air threat (US air defence view).** Convoys move at 15-20 mph, open column 50-100 m,
  closed 50 m or less. Columns are attacked "as targets of opportunity, rather than as preplanned targets". The expected
  attack: four aircraft low to avoid Hawk radar, a pop-up by one pair, a cluster-bomb laydown along the column by the
  other, then a re-attack on undamaged vehicles (`fm44_3_1981`, pp. 6-21 to 6-22). [1981]

### 2.7 NATO's own air defence: moving with the battle, and rebuilding it

- **ad-01 Hawk moves forward in bounds.** "The DS Hawk battalion supports division offensive operations by providing
  low- and medium-altitude day and night air defense for the attacking forces. To insure continuous support is
  provided, batteries of the Hawk battalion will leapfrog forward keeping advancing battalion task forces under their
  coverage" (`fm44_3_1981`, p. 7-5). [1981]
- **ad-02 Chaparral and Vulcan with columns.** Vulcan is integrated into the march column: at least two for a column of
  up to 1,000 m, at least three (normally a platoon of four) for 2-3 km. Redeye teams at the front and rear, less than
  3 km apart. "Since Chaparral cannot be fired while the carrier is moving", it is pre-positioned at bridges, crossroads
  and choke points along the route (`fm44_3_1981`, pp. 6-21 to 6-22). [1981]
- **ad-03 Early warning.** Forward area alerting radars (FAAR) cover at least 10 km beyond the fire units toward the
  threat, in pairs no more than 9 km apart, no closer than 2 km to the FEBA or line of contact; one stays in place while
  the other displaces (`fm44_3_1981`, pp. 6-11 to 6-12). The fire unit's display (TADDS) must be within 15 km of its FAAR
  (ch. 6, same section; exact page not pinned down). [1981]
- **ad-04 Rebuilding: what the sources say.** No period source read gives a time to rebuild a Hawk or Chaparral unit
  after losses. What exists:
  - a Hawk maintenance team carries "a battalion set of Hawk float equipment" (spare major items) for each Hawk battalion
    that needs it (`fm44_1_2_1984`, ch. on organisations, "Team EA, Hawk maintenance section");
  - for tanks, up to 80% of losses are reparable in about 10 man-hours (`fm_101_10_1_1976`, p. 7-16; no ADA equivalent
    found);
  - a SAMS monograph of December 1985: the war "will not allow for the full industrial capacity of the combatants to be
    harnessed. Therefore, each aircraft downed or ADA system destroyed represents a loss that can not be replaced";
    "a high number of operations will not have an AD umbrella because of initial losses inflicted upon NATO theater air
    defense forces" (`curran_1985_ada`, introduction). [1984-85]

## 4. Disagreements and which to use

1. **Force ratios.** FM 100-5 (1976) gives 3:1 and 6:1; FM 100-5 (1982) drops numbers for "intangible factors";
   Dupuy's posture factors make a prepared defence worth only 1.5 times. **Use:** 1976's ratios as the commander's
   decision rule (when to attack, hold or delay) and Dupuy's factors in the combat result. They answer different
   questions.
2. **Where the FSCL goes.** FM 6-20 (1977, change 1980): as close as possible; example 3 km. OTA (1987) on FOFA: about
   30 km. Jauron (1993): 1977 already let the ground commander set it deeper. **Use:** 20-30 km for 1985 (AirLand
   Battle and FOFA era), closer in a fast attack. Record both.
3. **Breakpoints.** Folklore 20-30% against Clark's "not supported by combat data" and HERO's mission-based endings.
   **Use:** ranges by mission, not a single threshold.
4. **Covering force duration.** 1976: 72 hours or seven days as examples. FM 101-10-1's example has the covering force
   losing 73% of its tanks on day 1 when heavily committed. **Use:** a covering force that is ordered to hold for days
   fights lightly committed and trades space; a heavily committed one is spent in a day.

## 5. Post-period items excluded, with dates

- FM 100-5 of 5 May 1986: just after the war's year; not read here; not used for numbers.
- FM 1-112 (1997 edition, superseding 1991): an attack helicopter battalion "can attack targets up to 150 km across the
  FLOT"; AH-64 combat radius about 150 km (ch. 1). Hindsight; the 1985 cross-FLOT depth is an open item.
- `helmbold_1990_caa_advance` (CAA-RP-90-1, June 1990): heavily and lightly engaged forces "stand still about 90 to 99
  percent of the time"; rates "practically independent of force ratios"; lognormal. Published after the period about
  historical data; used only for the shape of the distribution.
- `jauron_1993_fscl` (1993): FSCL history; used only as a pointer to FM 6-20 (1977).
- JCS Pub 1-02's later FSCL definition; the 1990s FSCL debates; kill boxes: 1991 and after.
- FM 101-10-1/2 (1987): searched for rates of advance; none found.
- Joint STARS, ATACMS and MLRS-delivered FOFA munitions: fielded after 1985 (planned in 1984-87).

- **ATP-27(B), *Offensive Air Support* (1979)**: the NATO text itself (depths of CAS, BAI, AI). Only US descriptions

- **US unit frontages in km for 1985**: a CGSC student text (e.g. ST 100-3, *Battle Book*, 1983-85) or FM 71-100
  (1990) would give them. The game uses SAM3D estimates until then.
- **The J-SAK concept (1982) and the J-SAK GOP (December 1984)**: not found online; read only through OTA and Davis.
- **Cross-FLOT attack helicopter depth and SEAD rules in 1984-86**: FM 1-112 (1986) or FM 17-50 (1984) not found.

  the TRADOC war-game basis for the 1976 ratios.
- **Dupuy, *Numbers, Predictions and War* (1979)**: not read directly; the posture factors are second-hand (1985
  edition via Ciano). His advance-rate tables in that book are not seen.
- **AR 220-1 (unit status ratings, early 1980s)**: peacetime readiness levels; not read.
- **Hawk and Chaparral emplacement and march-order times, and rebuild times**: FM 44-1-2's tables lost the values in

  Nations*, 1983 (unverified).
